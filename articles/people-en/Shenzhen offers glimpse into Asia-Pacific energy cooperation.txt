China Focus: Shenzhen offers glimpse into Asia-Pacific energy cooperation
SHENZHEN, Nov. 17 (Xinhua) -- At the press center for the 2026 APEC Economic Leaders' Week in Shenzhen, a large screen displays real-time data on the amount of green electricity powering the event and the carbon emissions avoided as a result.
During the event, key venues, transport hubs, and reception facilities are running on 100 percent green electricity. The electricity can be partly traced to offshore wind farms within south China's Guangdong Province, where Shenzhen is located, and partly to wind and solar bases in remote "desert, Gobi and barren land" regions in northwest China, among other sources.
As the 2026 APEC "China Year" is nearing its culmination, energy cooperation has emerged as one of its most tangible areas of progress.
Moreover, APEC economies account for more than 60 percent of global GDP and about 60 percent of global energy demand, making the region central to the global energy landscape.
Shenzhen, as the host city and a major innovation hub, offers a window into China's vision for an "Asia-Pacific Energy Community," a concept proposed and systematically defined for the first time in a joint statement issued at the 16th APEC Energy Ministerial Meeting held in September in Beijing. From low-carbon venues to daily conservation practices, the city provides tangible clues to understanding this vision.
At a garbage incineration park in the city's Nanshan District, municipal waste is transformed into electricity. The facility incinerates about 2,300 tonnes of waste each day, equivalent to about one-eighth of Shenzhen's daily total, converting heat into around 1.4 million kilowatt-hours of clean electricity per day.
This "Shenzhen solution" is now being adapted for other Asia-Pacific economies, including Thailand and Malaysia. Wang Kai, technological head of the Nanshan Energy Ecological Park, noted that Chinese teams have tailored the technology to local conditions, such as extending fermentation periods to handle higher moisture content in local waste and recycling incineration slag into building bricks.
Beyond garbage-to-electricity conversion, new energy vehicles rolling off production lines in Shenzhen, such as those produced by BYD, have powered the global shift toward electric mobility.
Moreover, Chinese firms are involved in energy conservation infrastructure construction in other countries in the Asia-Pacific region.
Guangdong-based China Southern Power Grid is taking part in a high-voltage direct current transmission project in Chile. Once completed, it will transmit clean wind and solar power from the Atacama Desert to load centers, helping resolve the mismatch between renewable generation and consumption.
Zhang Jingjia, an associate researcher at Nankai University's APEC research center, said the Asia-Pacific Energy Community will help forge connectivity across power grids, renewable energy supply chains, and AI-enabled energy systems.
Beyond cooperation initiatives, China's growing clean energy manufacturing capacity in general is helping lower the cost of green technologies and expand their availability worldwide.
According to the International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA), over the past decade, global wind and solar power costs have fallen by more than 60 percent and 80 percent, respectively, with much of this attributed to China's innovation, manufacturing and engineering endeavors.
China's growing contribution to global clean energy development is underpinned by the rapid transformation of its own energy system. In July 2026, China's installed solar capacity, for the first time in its history, overtook coal-fired capacity, making solar its largest power source by installed capacity.
"China is ready to work with all economies to turn our shared consensus into concrete action, transform our experience into collaboration, and jointly build an inclusive, innovative, and synergized Asia-Pacific energy community," said Wang Jianbo, deputy director of the National Energy Administration.
