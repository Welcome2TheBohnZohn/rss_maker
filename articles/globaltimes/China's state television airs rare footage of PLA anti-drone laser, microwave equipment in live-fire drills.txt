TITLE:
China's state television airs rare footage of PLA anti-drone laser, microwave equipment in live-fire drills

PUBLISHED:
2026-10-04 20:01:00

SOURCE:
Global Times

CATEGORY:

ARTICLE TEXT:
Counter-drone units of China’s People’s Liberation Army operate in combat-oriented training Photo: Screenshot of CCTV News
China's state television CCTV News on Sunday released rare footage of the People's Liberation Army (PLA) Army's counter-drone units in combat-oriented training, highlighting laser weapons, integrated missile-gun systems and high-power microwave weapons as an "iron triangle" of anti-drone equipment.
China's drone capabilities and counter-drone technologies are among the world's most advanced after years of development, Song Zhongping, a Chinese military affairs expert, told the Global Times on Sunday. He said the footage also showed how realistic training was helping refine the use of these weapons.
According to CCTV News, a brigade of the PLA's 75th Group Army conducted live-fire tactical drills involving multiple scenarios from daytime into the night, testing rapid response, firepower and sustained combat capabilities.
During the drills, CCTV News reported that air-defense units were assembled and deployed to designated positions after receiving orders to move. Radar then detected a dense swarm of drones approaching at extremely low altitude.
Counter-drone units activated ground-based laser and microwave systems, using optical dazzling and electromagnetic suppression to disrupt the swarm's formation. Interceptor drones took off to engage targets that broke through, followed by anti-aircraft gunfire against the remaining drones, CCTV reported.
Jia Jingcheng, a PLA Army serviceman quoted by CCTV, said the exercise focused on coordinating different types of air-defense equipment. It tested personnel's ability to assess complex aerial threats, respond to unexpected situations and combine multiple methods of attack to address weaknesses in combat readiness.
Immediately after the first engagement, exercise controllers declared that the troops' defensive position had been exposed, they were forced to withdraw their equipment, relocate and rapidly establish a new position, redeploying weapons and camouflage to restore their air defenses.
Radar units were networked to provide early warning and real-time tracking. As the exercise continued late into the night, units switched to nighttime operations, with radar, electro-optical and infrared equipment continuing to search for aerial targets, the report said.
He Qing, another PLA Army serviceman quoted by CCTV, said the drills combined responses to different aerial threats with battlefield maneuvering and the handling of unexpected situations in an unfamiliar, complex Gobi Desert environment. The aim was to strengthen rapid reaction, mobile defense and sustained readiness under both daytime and nighttime conditions.
Alongside the exercise footage, CCTV explained the complementary roles of the three types of counter-drone equipment. Laser weapons use high-energy beams to damage airborne drones, while integrated missile-gun systems provide terminal defense against small, slow and low-flying targets.
The missile-gun system shown in the footage features a centrally mounted six-barrel rotary cannon and four short-range air-defense missile launchers alongside it. It combines dense gunfire with precision missile strikes and can operate independently or as part of a networked air-defense system, according to CCTV.
The high-power microwave system shown uses an eight-wheel-drive chassis, with a large panel and a detection radar mounted at the rear. CCTV said the weapon releases microwave pulses that damage a drone's internal electronic components, causing it to fail and fall.
While lasers engage individual targets, microwave weapons can affect a wider area and engage multiple drones in a swarm, helping counter attacks designed to overwhelm defenses, the broadcaster said.
Supported by radar, electro-optical sensors and electronic detection equipment, these weapons form part of a layered defense system combining long-range early warning, electronic suppression and close-range interception, CCTV reported.
The development of such systems addresses challenges that inexpensive drones pose to conventional air defenses. Drones have relatively low barriers to development and procurement, allowing even countries and armed groups with limited technological capabilities to obtain them, Chinese military affairs expert Zhang Xuefeng was quoted by CCTV as saying.
Their low cost permits large-scale use, while their small size, low flight altitude and limited radar signatures make detection and interception difficult, Zhang said. Conventional defenses can consequently struggle to detect drones, intercept them affordably and stop them effectively.
Long-range air-defense missiles can cost millions of dollars each, while short-range missiles can cost tens or hundreds of thousands of dollars, Zhang noted. Using them against inexpensive drones creates an unfavorable cost exchange, compounded by limits on missile production. 
China has responded by developing a broad range of counter-drone systems and interception weapons, Song told the Global Times. He said the diversity and sophistication of its equipment reflected years of attention to both drone development and the threats drones create.
China had placed considerable emphasis on drones before the Russia-Ukraine conflict, and the conflict further deepened its understanding of their battlefield role, Song said. Alongside advances in drone applications, China has also prioritized the development and use of counter-drone equipment.
China's counter-drone equipment have developed alongside its drones, incorporating increasingly advanced technologies, Song said. He added that China would continue testing the equipment under realistic combat conditions to optimize its use.
