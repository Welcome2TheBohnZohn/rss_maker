TITLE:
Jurassic bird discovery by Chinese scientists challenges classic avian evolution view

ARTICLE TEXT:
A skeletal reconstruction of Yangyuanornis Photo: Courtesy of Wang Min
Chinese researchers have identified a new Jurassic bird fossil - a discovery that challenges the conventional assumptions that the origin and early evolution of birds were primarily driven by adaptations for flight, according to a report sent to the Global Times on Thursday by the Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology (IVPP) under the Chinese Academy of Sciences. 
The new findings demonstrate that acquisition of the bird body plan was not a systematic progression toward flight optimization, but a complex process involving the rapid exploration of different niches, including non-flying specialization, the report said. 
The new species also exhibits a striking combination of dinosaur-like and bird-like morphological features, researchers said in the report. 
The results of the research, conducted by a team led by researcher Wang Min at the IVPP in collaboration with the Institute of Geological Sciences in East China's Fujian Province, were published in Nature on Wednesday. 
The new bird was named 
Yuezhuornis yangyuanensis
, in Chinese "Yangyuan Yuezhuo," after Yangyuan township in Fujian, where the fossil was discovered. 
Yangyuanensis
 is derived from a mythical bird in ancient Chinese legends. It lived during the Late Jurassic period (150 million-148 million years ago). The crow-sized fossil represents the earliest known example of flight loss along the bird lineage, according to the report. 
An ecological reconstruction of Yuezhuornis yangyuanensis Photo: Courtesy of the Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology, Chinese Academy of Sciences
The discovery fills a critical gap in the temporal and evolutionary record of birds. As the earliest known flightless bird, it challenges the traditional paradigm that the avian body plan evolved almost exclusively under the selective pressures of flight. Instead, it exhibits a striking "mosaic" of primitive and derived features - blending classic avian traits with distinct characteristics inherited from non-avialan dinosaurs, Wang said. 
"When we examined 
Yuezhuornis
, we were struck by its extraordinary mix of features," said Wang, corresponding author of the study. "It possesses bird-like pelvic and hindlimb structures yet retains the specialized predatory foot and teeth of a small meat-eating dinosaur. It shows us that early bird evolution was far more complicated than we previously realized."
The fossil preserves closely packed, serrated teeth which are typically present in deinonychosaurs, but not birds. Most notably, Yuezhuornis possessed a hyperextensible, "sickle-clawed" second pedal digit - a classical feature of the raptor-like deinonychosaurs and troodontids, the report said. 
Researchers found that the new species falls outside the groups of tree-dwelling, aquatic and wading birds, instead clustering within the overlapping region of modern ground-dwelling birds and flightless birds. Combined with features such as a relatively wide angle between the clavicles and a trapezoidal coracoid, the findings indicate that it was a flightless, carnivorous bird adapted for running on land, according to the research team. 
The transition from terrestrial dinosaurs to flying birds is one of the most remarkable events in the history of life. Macroevolutionary models suggest that birds originated and underwent their early diversification during the Jurassic. However, the fossil record of birds from this pivotal geological period remains extremely sparse, leaving scientists without clear fossil evidence of how the characteristic avian body plan emerged, Wang said. 
For decades, a prevailing view has been that selective pressures associated with flight was the dominant force driving morphological evolution in early birds. Yet gaps in the fossil record at critical phylogenetic and chronological junctures have limited scientists' understanding of the evolutionary pathways and ecological diversity of birds in the earliest stages of their history, Wang noted. 
In the early stages of bird evolution, not all birds may have evolved skeletal structures in response to selective pressures favoring flight. Some groups may instead have been better adapted to ground-dwelling or other terrestrial lifestyles, but people simply had not found fossil evidence of them before. This suggests that ecological diversification during the early stages of bird evolution was richer and more complex than previously understood, he said.
