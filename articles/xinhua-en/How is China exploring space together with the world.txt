How is China exploring space together with the world?

ARTICLE TEXT:
BEIJING, Nov. 26 (Xinhua) -- From welcoming foreign astronauts aboard its space station to sharing lunar samples with scientists worldwide and providing satellite services across borders, China is steadily expanding international cooperation in space.
As its space program advances, China is making international collaboration an increasingly integral part of its efforts, creating more opportunities for countries to participate in space missions, share scientific resources and benefit from the peaceful use of space.
CREWED SPACEFLIGHT
On Wednesday, Pakistani astronaut Muhammad Zeeshan Ali joined Chinese astronauts Tang Hongbo and Wu Yi aboard the Shenzhou-24 spacecraft, which lifted off from the Jiuquan Satellite Launch Center in northwest China's Gobi Desert.
The spacecraft docked with the Tiangong space station at 11:56 a.m. Beijing time (0356 GMT) on Wednesday, according to the China Manned Space Agency (CMSA). Ali thus became the first foreign astronaut to board the Chinese space station and the first Pakistani to travel into space.
His participation in the mission followed an agreement on astronaut selection and training signed by China and Pakistan in February 2025, marking a new stage in bilateral space cooperation that has evolved from satellite development to crewed spaceflight.
According to a CMSA spokesperson, four China-Pakistan cooperative projects in biotechnology and materials will be carried out during the mission. Ali will also conduct an in-orbit experiment involving photosynthetic cyanobacteria.
On the significance of the mission, Ali said, "Now it is time to show this great achievement in orbit and send the world a message that we work hand in hand for the peaceful utilization of space."
Pakistani President Asif Ali Zardari said the historic cooperation demonstrated China's remarkable progress in space science and technology, its firm commitment to the peaceful use of outer space, and the significance of building a community with a shared future for humanity.
LUNAR EXPLORATION
International cooperation in China's space program extends beyond crewed missions to lunar exploration, where foreign partners are increasingly involved in both scientific missions and research.
Chang'e-4 and Chang'e-6 each carried four international payloads. The Chang'e-7 mission is set to carry six international scientific instruments developed by six countries and one international organization, while Chang'e-8 is expected to carry payloads involving 11 countries and regions and one international organization.
The two missions will also contribute to the development of the International Lunar Research Station, a scientific facility planned to include installations on the lunar surface, in lunar orbit and on Earth. So far, 17 countries and international organizations, along with more than 50 international research institutions, have joined the project.
Cooperation also extends to the sharing of scientific resources. In April 2025, the China National Space Administration (CNSA) announced that scientists from institutions in France, Germany, Japan, Pakistan, Britain and the United States had been granted access to lunar samples brought back by the Chang'e-5 mission in 2020 for research.
The opportunity has been welcomed by researchers involved in the program. Frederic Moynier, a cosmochemist at the Paris Institute of Planetary Physics, told the Science magazine that "China is setting a very positive example for scientific cooperation."
British planetary scientist Mahesh Anand of the Open University described the opportunity as "a great honour and privilege." He said he looked forward to studying the lunar material and "building new international collaborations and partnerships."
China's lunar exploration program has consistently upheld the principles of equality, mutual benefit, peaceful utilization and win-win cooperation, the CNSA said, adding that it will continue to accept international applications for lunar sample research in the hope that scientists worldwide will make discoveries that advance human knowledge and benefit humanity.
SATELLITE SERVICES
Beyond joint missions and scientific research, China is also sharing the practical benefits of space technology through satellite services that support everyday activities and help countries respond to natural disasters.
The BeiDou Navigation Satellite System (BDS) provides high-precision positioning services in fields such as transportation, agriculture and meteorological monitoring, with its products and services reaching more than 140 countries and regions.
The BDS has also been incorporated into the standards systems of 11 international organizations, including the International Civil Aviation Organization, the International Maritime Organization and COSPAS-SARSAT, an international satellite-based search and rescue system. Its applications span areas including civil aviation, maritime affairs and mobile communications.
China's Fengyun meteorological satellites, meanwhile, provide weather observations and data services to users worldwide, helping monitor tropical cyclones, heavy rainfall, wildfires and other extreme weather events.
Their contribution extends beyond national borders. Fengyun data are shared through the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) Integrated Global Observing System, supporting international weather forecasting and monitoring. WMO President Abdulla Al Mandous has described the satellites' observations as "critical, irreplaceable observations that bridge vital gaps in global climate and disaster monitoring networks."
The role of such services in disaster response was illustrated by a severe mudslide along the China-Nepal border in August. China's meteorological authorities provided Nepal with satellite data, precipitation monitoring and weather services to support disaster assessment, rescue operations and emergency response.
China's MAZU early-warning system also combines meteorological satellite data and AI forecasting models to support disaster warnings, offering more than 200 cloud-based early warning service products to over 50 countries.
In July, UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres described China as "an important partner" in global early-warning efforts, noting that its platforms, satellites and AI models were already helping dozens of countries strengthen their early-warning systems. ■
