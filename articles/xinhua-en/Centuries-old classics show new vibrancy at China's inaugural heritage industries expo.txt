Centuries-old classics show new vibrancy at China's inaugural heritage industries expo

ARTICLE TEXT:
HANGZHOU, Nov. 24 (Xinhua) -- In simple words, guests and visitors to China's inaugural heritage industries expo in Hangzhou, capital of east China's Zhejiang Province, summed up their impressions as "familiar yet surprised."
From celadon jewelry and latte-art tea to silk designed with AI, China's heritage industries have found a second wind, and the first Historical Classic Industries Expo, which was held from Friday to Sunday, is where the applause was landing.
Covering an exhibition area of about 30,000 square meters, the event drew more than 400 key enterprises and renowned brands. It highlighted innovation strategies across historical classic industries, including silk, tea, porcelain, and traditional Chinese medicine, all deeply rooted in Chinese culture and traditions dating back centuries or even millennia.
A large electronic jacquard loom, which can simultaneously control 42,000 warp threads at high density while significantly reducing labor costs, became an attention-grabber at the expo.
Automation and intelligent technologies are empowering a growing number of heritage industries. With AI design models, designers can quickly optimize silk and other product designs based on a massive pattern database and graphic algorithms, greatly shortening the design cycle, said Li Jianhua, chairperson of Hangzhou Wensli Silk Culture Co., Ltd.
He noted that Wensli Group's eco-friendly printing and dyeing equipment compresses more than a dozen traditional processes and a production line that once spanned about 5,000 square meters into a single machine of roughly 50 square meters, with virtually zero wastewater discharge.
At the exhibition area featuring the industries from east China's Jiangxi Province, staff demonstrated an intelligent capsule tea-and-coffee machine blending delicate latte art with traditional tea, drawing crowds eager for a taste.
"Historical classic industries must keep innovating based on tradition and develop products that better meet modern consumer needs," said Fu Zukang, an inheritor of yellow rice wine brewing techniques and Party chief of Kuaijishan Shaoxing Rice Wine Co., Ltd.
Fu added that modern equipment can now reproduce traditional brewing in a way better suited to health-conscious consumers, and that the sparkling yellow rice wine the company launched last year has been well received by younger consumers.
Guo Jian, director of the celadon and sword industries bureau of Longquan City in Zhejiang, shared a similar view, saying the city's heritage industries are "opening their arms" to modern life, shifting from "doing nothing but waiting for customers" to conducting demand-driven research and development.
According to the customs authorities in Lishui, which administers the city, Longquan sword-related exports reached 56.16 million yuan (about 8.37 million U.S. dollars) in the first nine months of this year, up 44.7 percent year on year. Rather than high-end collectible swords, anime and gaming props priced in the tens or hundreds of dollars are proving particularly popular among young people.
At the expo's interactive area, Nusrat Jahan Nipa, head of the Bangladesh Heritage Research and Professional Institute, made a creative silk umbrella for her friends back home. Describing herself as a Chinese traditional art enthusiast, she said the innovative hands-on experience on site was very novel and interesting.
China unveiled its first national policy document for centuries-old industries this year, as part of broader efforts to leverage the country's rich cultural heritage and build strong global brands.
By 2028, the country aims to cultivate 50 enterprises with annual revenues exceeding 10 billion yuan each, according to the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology.
The document also calls for 100 Chinese consumer brands and several industrial clusters with annual output exceeding 100 billion yuan to be developed by 2028. ■
