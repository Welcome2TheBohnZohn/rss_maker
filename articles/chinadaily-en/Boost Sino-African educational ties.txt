Boost Sino-African educational ties
The future of China-Africa relations hinges significantly on the collaborative efforts of our universities. Universities are not merely institutions that hand out degrees; they are epicenters of knowledge, developers of human capital, incubators of innovation and custodians of cultural heritage. Higher education is being reshaped by artificial intelligence, automation, biotechnology, climate change, renewable energy and the fourth industrial revolution, all of which are redefining the skills that societies need.
For Africa, these shifts present both opportunities and challenges. While the continent has the world's youngest population and enormous human potential, its universities still struggle due to limited access, quality issues, financial constraints, inadequate infrastructure and a widening gap between education and the labor market.
The challenge is no longer about producing more graduates, but about cultivating graduates who can generate knowledge, create employment and devise solutions.
China's remarkable transformation offers educational lessons. Decades of investment in education, science and technology have made Chinese universities global centers of innovation. Africa's task is not to duplicate that experience but to learn from it, adapt it and forge partnerships that leverage the unique strengths of both regions.
Historically, university cooperation has focused on exchanges, scholarships, conferences and memoranda of understanding. While these remain useful, the focus should now move from exchange to transformation, from mobility to joint knowledge creation, from joint research outputs to patents and commercialization, and from agreements on paper to practical partnerships.
We must explore what Chinese and African universities can achieve together that neither could do alone. We need partnerships that foster joint research programs, innovation hubs, laboratories, graduate schools and centers of excellence. This includes more joint PhDs and co-supervision, and deeper collaboration in AI, digital education, renewable energy, climate change, agriculture, health, manufacturing, engineering, entrepreneurship and the creative industries. The focus should be on African development priorities while addressing global challenges.
Tanzania illustrates these possibilities. Our relationship with China dates back to the early 1960s, marked by infrastructure projects such as the Tanzania-Zambia Railway and now spanning scholarships, research collaboration and institutional partnerships. At the University of Dar es Salaam, cooperation through the Confucius Institute has promoted academic exchange, people-to-people and cultural understanding and capacity building.
The lesson is clear: sustainable cooperation grows from mutual respect, shared interests and clearly defined practical outcomes. Future partnerships must evolve beyond traditional mobility and capacity-building toward co-creation and innovation — from student exchange to joint talent development, from academic visits to joint research, from technology transfer to joint innovation, from memoranda of understanding to measurable institutional outcomes, and from individual projects to strategic, long-term partnerships.
Talent development is a critical area where China and Africa must intensify cooperation. Africa needs young people equipped to work with emerging technologies, launch enterprises and solve complex problems; China brings experience in large-scale human-capital development, technological education and innovation ecosystems. Our universities should build joint talent platforms linking students, academics, industry and government, with reciprocal mobility, joint master's and doctoral programs and blended-learning arrangements. The aim is a new generation of professionals who understand both societies.
Digital transformation is another major frontier. China has deep experience in digital infrastructure, educational technology and large-scale online learning; African universities will benefit if cooperation addresses our realities: connectivity, affordability, devices, skills and the urban-rural divide. We should create joint China-Africa digital education platforms for cross-continental teaching and resource sharing.
AI should also be a major area of collaboration. However, as we embrace AI, we must address ethics, academic integrity, data governance, privacy, equity and the responsible use of technology. Universities on both sides should develop technological capacity and ethical frameworks together.
Partnerships must be judged by the knowledge and solutions they produce. China and Africa should define joint research agendas where interests converge: climate change and environmental sustainability; renewable energy and the energy transition; food security and agricultural transformation; public health; digital transformation and AI; critical minerals and value addition; manufacturing and industrialization; urbanization and sustainable cities; education and skills development; entrepreneurship and employment creation. The principle is simple: we should research jointly, and translate that into joint publications, patents, innovations, enterprises and policy solutions.
China-Africa cooperation cannot be a one-way model of transfer. Our universities should embrace knowledge co-creation, with African and Chinese scholars as equal partners in defining questions, designing methods and translating findings into practice. The goal should not be "What can China do for Africa?" or "What can Africa do for China?", but "What can China and Africa do together for both and for humanity?"
The next generation of cooperation should move beyond a collection of agreements toward a more integrated model that builds deeper strategic partnerships, brings researchers, students, industry and policymakers together through joint centers of excellence, and develops talent through scholarships, joint degrees, doctoral training, co-supervision and reciprocal mobility. The goal is to turn these networks and skills into joint innovation, translating research into products, enterprises, technologies and services. Ultimately, cooperation should be judged by what it produces — graduates, innovations, enterprises and practical solutions — rather than by the number of agreements signed or delegations exchanged.
The frontiers of educational development are no longer bounded by individual institutions or national borders. They are defined by our capacity to connect knowledge, people and technology across them. Higher education must become a more strategic pillar of the China-Africa relationship. For Tanzania, we remain committed to strengthening cooperation with China not as a recipient of aid, but as a partner with whom we can co-create knowledge, develop talent and advance innovation.
China-Africa university cooperation should move from partners in exchange to partners in development, from recipients of knowledge to co-creators of knowledge, and from isolated institutions into interconnected engines of transformation. As the Father of the Nation of Tanzania, Julius Nyerere, emphasized in his address, "the role of universities", more than six decades ago, universities in developing countries must not simply receive knowledge; they must contribute to it as well.
His vision underscores the importance of mutual contribution and knowledge creation, a principle that remains central to China-Africa university cooperation today. Our greatest achievement will not be stronger universities but a generation of young Africans and Chinese equipped to build a more prosperous, innovative and shared future.
The author is the vice-chancellor of the University of Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. The article is adapted from his keynote speech at the China-Africa University Presidents Forum on Frontiers of Education Development.
