NZ faces deadlock with election results

ARTICLE TEXT:
New Zealand faces uncertainty over its next government after the release of its general election final results on Friday, with neither the ruling coalition nor the main opposition securing a majority, raising the possibility of a return to the polls.
The center-right National Party, led by Prime Minister Christopher Luxon, secured 40 of the 130 seats in Parliament, according to the country's Electoral Commission.
The three-party governing coalition also includes ACT New Zealand, which had 13 seats, and the New Zealand First Party, which had 12 seats.
The left-leaning opposition Labour Party secured 36 seats and, together with the 17 seats of the Green Party, seven seats of the Opportunity Party and five seats of Te Pati Maori, means the total of 65 seats equals the coalition's number which falls short of the parliamentary majority needed to govern.
Coalitions are common under New Zealand's electoral system. The results mean Luxon will have to turn to the Opportunity Party to break through and avoid another election, according to public broadcaster Radio New Zealand.
RNZ quoted Luxon as saying that "one of the strongest messages" he heard throughout this campaign is that voters "want more cross-party collaboration".
"They want their leaders to find common ground and to get on with delivering for them," he said.
"I've heard the message, and I'll be taking that into these negotiations," Luxon said, adding that they would be challenging and take time.
The results can still be challenged with recounts, with a single seat change set to affect the governing outcome, according to the broadcaster.
The existing government will remain in caretaker mode until the next administration is sworn in, with Parliament required to meet by Jan 14, it said.
Opportunity Party leader Qiulae Wong said after the release of the final results that her party would seek to form a stable government but did not rule out another election, saying negotiations with other parties are still needed.
Pledging kinder, gentler politics as well as action on climate change, Wong's party became the first in decades to form Parliament without a former cabinet minister as leader.
She has vowed to work with whichever party has the largest number of seats. But she has insisted her party's "bottom lines" on climate change action, cost of living and cleaning up politics are nonnegotiable.
Speaking outside Parliament following the result, she told journalists she expected "challenging" talks.
She preaches "pragmatic politics" as a counter to growing polarization, saying she wants to "bring the heat down a little bit and actually just focus on moving the country forward".
New Zealand uses a mixed-member proportional system to elect its Parliament. Voters cast two ballots, one for a political party and another for a candidate in their local electorate. Under the system, governments are typically formed by two or more parties that collectively command a majority in Parliament.
