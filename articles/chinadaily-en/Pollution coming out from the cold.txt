TITLE:
Pollution coming out from the cold

ARTICLE TEXT:
The Antarctic is often perceived as the last clean place on Earth — a continent so distant from cities, factories and highways that it seems almost untouched by modern civilization. However, white does not always mean clean.
The ice and snow of Antarctica have been quietly keeping a record of humanity's environmental footprint. They preserve traces of changing temperatures, precipitation and atmospheric chemistry. Among those traces is mercury, a pollutant that can travel across borders and oceans and settle thousands of kilometers from its original source.
For decades, the frozen world has acted like a vast cold-storage facility for that pollution. But this cold storage is not permanent.
A recent study published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences reveals what happens when this frozen archive begins to thaw. Scientists examined layers of sediment from the seabed around the Antarctic Peninsula — natural time capsules that preserve traces of pollution year after year.
By reading those layers, they were able to reconstruct how mercury has moved through the region over the past 200 years or so.
Their findings are alarming. Mercury is building up on the Antarctic Peninsula's continental shelf at nearly twice the global continental shelves rate. Since industrialization began, the mercury accumulation rate has increased by about 160 percent. Even in one of the world's most isolated regions, the footprint of human pollution is growing.
While most of the mercury reaches the seafloor from the atmosphere, a significant portion also comes from land. That land-based mercury includes both naturally occurring mercury and pollution deposited decades ago and stored in soils, ice and other parts of the landscape.
This is an important distinction, which tells us that some of the mercury now entering the Antarctic environment is not new pollution.
It is old pollution re-entering the cycle. Pollution does not disappear merely because it has been buried, frozen or otherwise stored.
Climate change is turning long-term storage into a source of renewed pollution.
As temperatures rise and glaciers retreat, previously covered land is being exposed and mercury accumulated in ice, soils and other terrestrial environments is being reintroduced into coastal waters.
The study estimates that mercury released from land as a result of ice melting has increased by about 550 percent since industrialization, while mercury exchange between the ocean and atmosphere has risen by about 350 percent.
It's not that Antarctica has suddenly begun manufacturing mercury, but the mercury emitted in the past is being put back into circulation.
That is perhaps the more troubling dimension of climate change. Global warming not only creates new environmental risks but also reactivates old ones. Historically, pollution policy has focused on current emissions. But the Antarctic story suggests that we must also consider what has already accumulated in nature's warehouse.
The issue extends far beyond mercury. Glaciers, permafrost, soils and sediments can all serve as long-term repositories for pollutants.
As the climate warms — and as fires, floods, erosion and land disturbance become more consequential — some of that stored pollution may be released again. Chemicals emitted generations ago can become today's environmental problem.
We should not mistake a warning sign for a complete ecological disaster. Once mercury enters the ocean, it can settle with particles, move with currents or return to the atmosphere.
Under certain microbial and chemical conditions, inorganic mercury can transform into methylmercury, a more biologically available form that can enter food webs.
Determining how much of the newly released mercury ultimately reaches marine organisms requires sustained monitoring and ecological research.
Climate policy and pollution control are not separate agendas.
While reducing new mercury emissions is essential, governments must also assess the pollution already stored in natural systems and how climate change could destabilize those reservoirs.
The Minamata Convention on Mercury and global efforts to reduce greenhouse-gas emissions should be seen as interconnected commitments aimed at preventing yesterday's pollution and today's warming from compounding each other.
There is also a question of fairness. Developing countries face challenges in energy security, employment and basic livelihoods.
A cleaner transition requires access to affordable technologies, financing, monitoring equipment and technical expertise, not simply demands for rapid change.
Global environmental governance should help countries reduce pollution without simply shifting high-emitting industries and environmental risks from one part of the world to another.
Science also needs to broaden its perspective. Long-term monitoring should connect the atmosphere, ice, meltwater, seawater, sediments and living organisms, enabling researchers to track not only where pollutants go but also when they return.
Antarctica is thousands of kilometers from many of the world's major sources of industrial pollution. Yet mercury has reached its shores, been stored and may now be released again as the climate warms.
That should change our perception of both pollution and climate change. The planet has no true boundaries. Pollution can travel far beyond borders, and time does not necessarily render it harmless.
What we bury in ice today may not remain there tomorrow.
Antarctica is not a world apart. It is a mirror — and the longer we ignore what it is reflecting, the harder it will be to escape the consequences.
Liu Maodian is a researcher at the College of Urban and Environmental Sciences, Peking University; and Zhou Chengzhen is a postdoctoral fellow at the same institute.
