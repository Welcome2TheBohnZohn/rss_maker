From fading mining town to Xinjiang ski destination

ARTICLE TEXT:
When 13-year-old Qadir Hali arrived in Koktokay in the Xinjiang Uygur autonomous region in 1942, he could scarcely have imagined that the mining town would one day become a destination for skiers from across China and beyond.
More than eight decades later, his son Kumash Qadir, now 63, has witnessed that transformation firsthand.
Once a major mining center, Koktokay, in Xinjiang's Altay prefecture, is now home to one of China's earliest-opening ski resorts. Since launching its 2026-27 season on Oct 20, the resort has welcomed nearly 25,000 visitors, including more than 22,000 skiers, in the first two weeks.
Mines fade, slopes rise
Koktokay, meaning "green forest" in Kazakh, sits in the mineral-rich Altay Mountains. After mines were discovered in 1935, Koktokay earned a reputation as a major industrial center.
But declining reserves and changing market demand brought economic hardship from the mid-to-late 1980s. The town's population fell from a peak of 50,000 to 60,000 to just over 10,000.
Kumash, who was born in Koktokay, followed his father into the mining industry and worked there for 20 years. He recalled a thriving town where sporting events were regularly held.
In 1999, as the mining industry declined, he lost his job.
"As a local who grew up here, I felt quite lonely at the time and had little certainty about the future. Some people urged me to move away, but I had grown up here and felt attached to my hometown, so I wanted to stay," he said.
To make a living, Kumash bought a car and began driving passengers between Koktokay and Urumqi, the regional capital. "Later the ski resort was built, and things slowly began to change," he recalled.
The Koktokay International Ski Resort opened in 2020, providing Kumash with a new job on its operations team.
His working day begins before dawn, when he drives his pickup truck to deliver safety nets to the slopes and anti-slip mats to the ski hall. In his spare time, he even helps care for a husky left with him by a skier.
"Now I have a job, and the basics of life are taken care of," he said. "In the past, herders had little to do in winter, and the villages and town were quiet. Now there is winter work. Some people work at the resort, some serve tourists, and some rent out their houses for extra income."
The tourism boom has also created opportunities for younger residents.
Ardak Salinbek, 28, works at a ski equipment shop at an altitude of 2,200 meters, serving nearly 100 customers a day in winter.
Born in Kairti Village, 15 kilometers from the resort, to a herder father and a farmer mother, she initially worked at a township hospital after graduating from medical school, earning less than 3,000 yuan ($425) a month.
"The pay wasn't high, and the work was rather dull. I felt the ski-gear business suited me better. I get to meet friends from all over the country and learn something new every day," she said.
Her parents initially questioned the career change but were convinced when she brought home her first monthly paycheck of 16,000 yuan. "As skiing in Koktokay became better known across the country, my parents realized it was a new opportunity for young people. They told me to 'go for it'," Salinbek recalled.
"Skiing has changed my hometown beyond recognition, and I want to contribute to its development," she said. "For me, that means finding the right gear for every skier who comes."
