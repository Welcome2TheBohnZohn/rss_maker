Chinese meteorological and water authorities have stepped up cooperation with Nepal following the deadly mudslide that struck the China-Nepal border, sharing monitoring data and providing weather and hydrological forecasts to support rescue efforts.
The Aug 26 disaster originated on the Nepalese side of the border and severely affected Gyirong Port in the Xizang autonomous region. The area's high altitude, complex terrain and rapidly changing weather conditions have made rescue operations particularly challenging.
"From late August to early September, plateau shear lines and low vortices are frequently active along the border, with frequent precipitation, posing serious challenges to rescue work," said Ma Xuekuan, chief forecaster at the National Meteorological Center.
Following a request from Nepal's Department of Hydrology and Meteorology around noon on Aug 26, the China Meteorological Administration organized an emergency joint consultation with its Nepalese counterpart that afternoon. A second consultation was held on Aug 31, according to Zhang Tianhang, a senior engineer at the World Meteorological Center, Beijing.
The two sides also established a WeChat working group for real-time information sharing, allowing them to respond quickly to requests during the emergency.
The consultations used MAZU, a cloud-based early warning system, to jointly assess weather conditions and forecast precipitation in the affected area.
China subsequently established a dedicated meteorological support team for Nepal and began providing daily monitoring and forecasting reports in English.
"Overall, we have provided seamless forecasting services covering the next 30 days, offering strong support for Nepal's rescue operations," Zhang said.
China has also provided Nepal with a customized version of MAZU. Nepal has since requested additional functions, including lightning monitoring, subseasonal-to-seasonal forecasts and more detailed monitoring and forecasting products.
Chinese authorities have shared a range of meteorological products and observation data through the customized version of MAZU, Zhang said.
Water authorities have also joined the effort.
A barrier lake formed near the confluence of Nepal's Cuojian River and China's Purepu Tsangpo River following the disaster. The Ministry of Water Resources immediately sent a hydrological emergency monitoring team to the area, working with Xizang's regional hydrological and water resources survey authorities.
Chinese authorities are providing Nepal with daily updates in five areas, including the development of the barrier lake; potential chain risks from impact pits along the Cuojian River; risks from glacial lakes in the Gyirong Zangbo basin; the development and risk forecasts of newly formed barrier lakes; and emergency hydrological monitoring information.
The information is intended to help Nepal assess risks and organize rescue efforts downstream, according to the ministry.
Following the disaster, the CMA mobilized resources from the national to local levels. A small ground weather station was installed near the disaster area on the night of Aug 26, and a mobile weather radar was deployed nearby on Sept 1.
"These facilities have supplemented our understanding of local weather conditions and improved the precision and accuracy of local forecasts," Ma said.

GYIRONG, Xizang -- Road access to the core area of the mudslide-hit Gyirong Port in Southwest China's Xizang autonomous region was basically restored at 10 am on Wednesday after days of round-the-clock repair work on the damaged G216 national highway.
Rescue teams, relief supplies and some rescue equipment have entered the core rescue area via this road, rescuers said.
A section of the G216 national highway, the only land route to Gyirong Port, was severely damaged by the mudslide carrying huge boulders, with the restoration of road access critically important in aiding large-scale search operations in the core disaster area.
The repair operation, notably, posed formidable challenges.
"Conditions vary from one section of the road to another," said Zhou Gang from China Anneng, a state-owned engineering and rescue enterprise. The original roadbed remained usable in some sections, but most of it had been severely damaged.
The work zone lay in a narrow mountain gorge, with steep mountainsides on one side and a fast-flowing river on the other. This confined space made it difficult for multiple pieces of heavy machinery to operate simultaneously, severely hampering the work, Zhou added.
An even greater challenge came from the unpredictable conditions inside the gorge, featuring fast-flowing water and lingering risks of rockfalls, landslides and other secondary hazards.
Crews responded by adapting their methods. Along narrow sections, multiple excavators worked in relay, lining up one behind another to dig and pass debris from one machine to the next.
In sections with the strongest currents, excavators pushed into the water to open the way, while crews built diversion structures and used rock-filled steel cages to stabilize the roadbed, advancing section by section.
The mudslide, caused by an ice-rock avalanche in Nepal, hit Gyirong Port on Aug 26, claiming 16 lives and leaving 546 people missing at the border port as of 6 pm on Saturday, according to the regional government's latest update.
Following the disaster, Xizang swiftly activated its highest-level emergency response for natural disasters, mobilizing rescue forces from across the region for search and rescue operations, road clearance and other relief work in Gyirong.

By the time Phuntsok Drakpa sat down to talk about the rescue operation at Gyirong Port in the Xizang autonomous region on Tuesday, he still had not dealt with the insect that had burrowed into his skin days earlier.
He had more urgent things to do.
Phuntsok Drakpa, an officer at a community police station in Gyirong county, immediately volunteered to join the search-and-rescue effort after a deadly mudslide struck the port on Aug 26.
The journey from Lapher village, where his police station is located, to the port was not a road in the conventional sense. Much of it was a narrow trail cut into the mountainside, with a cliff on one side and a drop so deep that the bottom could not be seen, he said. In some places, there was no trail at all.
The officers had to push through dense brush, cutting branches, thorny vines and weeds to make their way forward. About 1.5 kilometers from the port, they reached the most dangerous section: a valley more than 200 meters deep, with slopes of 60 to 70 degrees.
"Some sections were bare rock. Others were wet and slippery from the rain. We had to use ropes to lower ourselves down," he said.
Phuntsok Drakpa had one thought as he moved: Get there as quickly as possible.
"If we arrived one minute earlier, there could be one more chance," he said.
The officers traveled about 7.7 km on foot. About 5.5 km could be covered at a slow jog, while the rest was so difficult to traverse that it consumed most of their time. The journey lasted more than 10 hours. They left at 9 am on Saturday and returned by helicopter the following morning, he recalled.
The mountain posed hazards beyond the disaster itself. There were leeches and poisonous snakes, as well as insects that lived in pine trees. One of them had burrowed into his skin, leaving a wound he was still carrying when he gave the interview.
The officers covered themselves as tightly as they could, fastening the cuffs of their uniforms to keep the insects out.
They slept together outdoors along the route. When they woke the next morning, they found a bear's paw print about two meters from where they had been sleeping.
"At the time, nobody had time to be frightened. We simply kept moving," he said.
From 9 am one day until noon the next, the officers ate nothing. At one point, they encountered firefighters and gave much of their food to them.
"Yet I did not feel hungry," he said.
They spent a night in the rain. He did not feel cold, either, though he knew that under normal circumstances he would have.
Once they arrived at the port, the task became harder to describe.
"The air smelled of decay. The site was completely quiet, without any sound."
The officers searched among the rocks, calling out and looking for any sign that someone might be trapped beneath them — a shoe, a piece of clothing, anything that might suggest where a person was.
"It was very difficult," Phuntsok Drakpa said. "There was nothing we could do except call out and look under the rocks."
If they found something, they dug. Sometimes, all they could do was cry, he said.
But there was no time to dwell on what they had seen. After the interview, Phuntsok Drakpa planned to head to Salhe township, taking the same mountain route again.
He still had not dealt with the insect in his skin.
"I plan to buy some insecticide and spray it so that the insect will come out on its own," he said.

For Tashi Norbu's 61-year-old father from Lapher village in Gyirong county, Xizang autonomous region, starting the day with tsampa — roasted barley flour mixed with yak butter and tea — does more than nourish the body. It soothes the heart and sets a peaceful tone for the day ahead.
So when Tashi Norbu and his family were relocated to Jephuk village following the mudslide that struck the Gyirong border port on Aug 26, they found comfort in the familiar food provided at their temporary shelter, as well as in the free accommodation.
"The streets here are narrower and the houses are closer together than at our home. But the altitude of Jephuk village is similar to that of our village, so we adapted to the new environment pretty quickly," he said.
About 160 villagers from Lapher and Sichung have been resettled in rural homestays in Jephuk village, a popular tourist destination with dozens of guesthouses and restaurants.
"My father felt carsick at first during the bumpy ride here, but he feels much better after seeing a doctor arranged by relief workers and volunteers," Tashi Norbu said. "The restaurant near our temporary home sometimes brings Tibetan noodle soup to our door in the morning as a gesture of kindness."
"Despite the disaster, we feel well taken care of," he said.
According to local authorities, 499 residents from five affected villages have been relocated to five temporary shelters converted from hotels and homestays following the devastating mudslide.
Jampal Dorje, a staff member at the shelter in Jephuk village, said many of the relocated residents are elderly and have been housed with their children to ensure they receive proper care.
"Most elderly residents are not used to rice and steamed buns included in the standard food supplies, so we have requested more tsampa, Tibetan fried noodles and dumplings from the supply reserves in recent days," he said.
Jampal Dorje said staff members now visit households every day to keep track of residents' needs, paying particular attention to their health, sleep quality and other basic daily necessities.
Several medical workers are stationed at the shelter to provide care, while designated vehicles are available to transport residents with more serious symptoms to hospitals, he said.

Rescuers are intensifying search operations around Gyirong Port while working to reopen the main ground route to the disaster-hit area, as difficult terrain, heavy rainfall and the risk of secondary disasters continue to complicate rescue efforts.
As of 6 pm on Tuesday, crews clearing National Highway G216 had advanced to within 700 meters of fully reopening the route. The highway was severely damaged by a massive mudslide that struck the area on Aug 26 after originating on the Nepalese side of the China-Nepal border.
The disaster caused major casualties and left many people missing, while roads, electricity and communications were also disrupted.
On Tuesday morning, memorial ceremonies for the victims were held at the core rescue area and road-clearing sites near Gyirong Port, attended by rescuers, local officials and residents.
Norbu Tsering, acting mayor of Shigatse, said rescuers had spent four consecutive days searching about 0.7 square kilometers around the border gate using life-detection equipment and other methods.
"The priority remains reopening the road to the border gate while further intensifying the search in the core area," he said.
Search operations are advancing from both directions. Firefighters are moving from the outer edge of the debris field toward Gyirong Port, while teams in the core area are searching outward. A stretch of about 2 km has so far been searched from the outer edge toward the port.
Nearly 700 personnel and more than 300 sets of machinery have been involved in restoring the land route, while supplies and additional rescue forces are being sent into the core area by air.
Large machinery is also advancing toward the core area along G216. Thubten Nyima, a firefighter with the Xizang fire and rescue brigade, said firefighters are preparing to combine mechanized excavation with more detailed manual searches once heavy equipment reaches areas where deeper debris cannot be effectively searched by hand.
The same terrain that amplified the mudslide's destructive force is now hampering rescue work. Experts said the disaster's impact was intensified by the steep elevation drop, high speed and narrow valley. The debris flow traveled about 22 km at an average speed of around 50 meters per second, gathering loose material as it descended.
Heavy machinery can only advance along the narrow valley, while search, road repair and debris-clearing teams must share the same confined corridor.
Thubten Nyima said reaching the core area had already placed heavy physical demands on rescuers, while damaged transportation and communications infrastructure created further difficulties. Deep mud has slowed movement and reduced the effectiveness of some commonly used detection equipment.
The mud in some areas is knee-or even waist-deep, he said. Teams have had to identify relatively stable routes and adjust search plans according to terrain, weather conditions and the likely direction of the mudslide. Rain can further complicate operations, requiring plans to be continuously adjusted.
Conditions along G216 remain difficult, with unstable cliffs on one side and fast-flowing water on the other. Currents reach 6 to 7 meters per second in some sections.
Lin Zhenkui, a technical expert with China Anneng Group, told CCTV that the mudslide raised the riverbed and altered the channel, forcing water onto parts of the former roadway.
In some sections, crews are effectively building a new road through the water rather than simply clearing debris. Large rocks, reinforced stone cages and diversion structures are being used to stabilize the roadbed and reduce the force of the current.
Communications and power supplies are also being restored. As of 5 pm on Tuesday, telecommunications operators had repaired one damaged base station and set up 12 emergency stations, according to the regional communications administration.
State Grid said power crews were working around the clock to support road-clearing operations, with power lines extended to within 1.6 km of Gyirong Port.
Secondary hazards remain a major concern.
Three national emergency rescue teams totaling 39 personnel have been deployed with slope radar, drones and life-detection equipment to monitor potential landslides, collapses and barrier lakes, according to the Ministry of Emergency Management.
China Anneng Group is also using laser-based video, artificial intelligence monitoring equipment and drones to track slope changes around the clock.
The Ministry of Water Resources said on Tuesday that 14 previously identified glacial lakes remained relatively stable, though glaciers around an impact crater near the source area in Nepal could still collapse, posing a significant risk.
Three rope-ladder evacuation routes totaling about 180 meters have also been installed along G216, allowing rescuers to reach higher ground quickly in the event of sudden flooding or another mudslide.
Norbu Tsering said authorities are maintaining contact with the families of those missing and providing updates on rescue efforts.
