Moon rocks a symbol of China's global vision

ARTICLE TEXT:
One of the most memorable advertising tag lines in the United States was "You've come a long way, baby". The phrase can also be used to describe China's meteoric rise in the past few decades. Nothing proves this point better than a recent ceremony at the United Nations headquarters in Vienna, Austria, where China presented moon rocks and soil samples from the far side of the Moon for permanent display. The out-of-this-world gift has transformed China's scientific achievement into a lasting diplomatic symbol at the heart of multilateralism.
The Chang'e-6 lunar mission returned to Earth with almost two kilograms of lunar material in June 2024, marking the first and only successful retrieval of samples from the Moon's far side.
The complex mission required advanced robotics, autonomous spacecraft operations and a relay satellite to communicate with Earth despite the Moon blocking direct radio contact. The samples are both a unique scientific treasure and evidence of China's advancing technological capabilities.
At the Vienna ceremony, Shan Zhongde, the administrator of the China National Space Administration, emphasized China's commitment to sharing the benefits of space exploration internationally and promoting peaceful use of space.
Monica Juma, director-general of the UN Office at Vienna, highlighted the connection between scientific achievement and international cooperation, underscoring Vienna's role as a world capital where science and diplomacy meet.
Aarti Holla-Maini, director of the UN Office for Outer Space Affairs, noted that the gift serves as a teacher, not a trophy, emphasizing that the rare lunar samples from the far side, thanks to China, belong to everyone.
Li Song, China's permanent representative to the UN and other international organizations in Vienna, also shared his views during an interview at the event. He said that the event demonstrates China's commitment and actions to firmly uphold the status and role of the UN in collaboration with all parties, improve global governance of outer space and better benefit all of humanity.
Together, these remarks framed the samples not merely as a national prize, but as an invitation for all nations to participate in humanity's exploration of the universe.
The ceremony reminded me of a historical parallel that underscores China's remarkable journey. In the early 15th century, admiral Zheng He of the Ming Dynasty (1368-1644) led treasure ships across the Indian Ocean, carrying envoys and gifts to foreign rulers. Some experts even suggest that Zheng visited the Americas before Christopher Columbus and circumnavigated the globe a century before Ferdinand Magellan.
Six centuries ago, Zheng's fabled voyages projected Chinese maritime power, cultivated diplomatic relationships and brought extraordinary objects, such as giraffes from East Africa, back to China. These were seen as auspicious Chinese unicorns or qilin, symbolizing the prestige of Zheng's diplomatic exchanges.
The underlying diplomatic mechanism resonates through the ages. Just as in the past, China's contemporary achievements have produced extraordinary objects that communicate capability and prestige. Zheng's treasure fleet was part of the message. Today, the Chang'e missions are the 21st century equivalent, with lunar samples serving as extraordinary objects from a quarter-million-mile journey.
But there is a striking difference. Zheng demonstrated China's ability to reach distant parts of the known world, while Chang'e-6 demonstrated its ability to retrieve material from an unexplored region of another world. The frontiers of discovery have shifted from the Earth's oceans to the Moon's surface and beyond.
Retrieving lunar material requires advanced launch systems, robotics, communications and autonomous operations. The samples convey two messages: China's capabilities and its willingness to share a finite, precious resource with the international community.
The Vienna display serves as a permanent reminder that an achievement of one country can become a shared interest. It also demonstrates how a small quantity of material can carry diplomatic significance far beyond its physical size. This gift to the UN in Vienna also embodies China's commitment to a community with a shared future for humanity.
From Zheng's treasure fleets to Chang'e's journey to the Moon's far side, the principle remains the same: extraordinary achievements can open diplomatic doors.
The Chang'e-6 lunar samples gifted to the UN serve as more than symbols of China's scientific achievement. They also echo the spirit of the Global Development, Global Security, Global Civilization and Global Governance Initiatives — reminding us that humanity's greatest achievements are those that expand our shared horizons, transcend national boundaries and benefit all humanity.
China's out-of-this-world win-win space diplomacy continues to reach new heights with the successful Nov 25 launch of the Shenzhou XXIV spaceflight mission. It marks the participation of the first foreign astronaut aboard the Tiangong space station, Pakistani Muhammad Zeeshan Ali.
