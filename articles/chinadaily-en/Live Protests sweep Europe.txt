A weekend cyberattack on refrigeration systems at French blood storage facilities attempted to raise temperatures to potentially dangerous levels, authorities disclosed on Wednesday.
Three cold storage sites operated by the French Blood Establishment, or EFS, in the Burgundy-Franche-Comte region of east-central France, were affected after hackers breached systems run by Coolworld, a Dutch refrigeration and remote monitoring provider.
The attack was first reported on Wednesday by Le Canard Enchaine, a French weekly newspaper, which said temperature settings on units storing plasma and blood samples were reprogrammed from around -30 C to 110 C — well above boiling point.
Officials stressed these were target settings entered into the system, not actual temperatures reached inside storage rooms. Workers averted disaster after a night-shift technician spotted the anomaly and moved quickly to transfer or secure affected products.
EFS President Frederic Pacoud said the attack could have resulted in the loss of plasma destined for patients, but a warning system ensured "there was no compromise to the safety of blood products".
No health consequences or product losses were reported, and distribution operations continued uninterrupted, an EFS statement said.
"Monitoring and alarm systems quickly detected the anomalies, allowing employees to take action to ensure that no blood products or samples were compromised," it added.
Authorities have since disabled remote access to the compromised units. The Health Ministry credited monitoring and alarm systems with protecting blood supplies from damage.
On Wednesday, Coolworld acknowledged the incident, saying it had "responded promptly by implementing the appropriate measures". The company attributed the security flaw to third-party software.
"The vulnerability currently being reported in the media concerns a component supplied by a third-party provider and not directly controlled by Coolworld," it said.
Operations across five countries — France, Germany, Ireland, the Netherlands, and the United Kingdom — fall under Coolworld's remit.
Investigators from France's national cybersecurity agency and Health Ministry are probing the breach, but said no suspects have been identified.
Similar intrusions struck refrigeration infrastructure elsewhere across Europe last month. Freezers and refrigerators were compromised at supermarkets and independent stores across France, Belgium, and the Netherlands in mid-October, spoiling large quantities of food.
Ahold Delhaize, the Dutch retail giant hit in that wave, warned Wednesday the damage could cut fourth-quarter sales by up to $281 million and reduce operating income by more than $112 million.
"While most of the disruption was contained within two weeks, work continues to fully restore our normal operations," the retailer's CEO Frans Muller said. In the United States, Pentagon officials are investigating cyberattacks in August that hit food storage coolers at several military bases, destroying an undisclosed amount of supplies.
PARIS -- Around 600,000 people took part in demonstrations across France on Thursday, French television channel BFM TV reported, citing an initial estimate by the French Interior Ministry.
However, the General Confederation of Labour (CGT) estimated that nearly 1.6 million people took part in Thursday's demonstrations nationwide, including 350,000 in Paris, according to BFM TV.
According to figures released by the French Ministry of National Education, 16 percent of teachers were on strike on Thursday. Meanwhile, nearly 33 percent of higher education institutions were disrupted by the movement as of midday, affecting 60 of the country's 184 institutions.
Nearly 10 percent of employees in the French state civil service took part in Thursday's strike over salaries and purchasing power, according to figures released by the Ministry of Public Action and Accounts.
Transport services were generally maintained, French Transport Minister Philippe Tabarot said in a post on social media platform X, adding that as of 2 pm (1300 GMT), nine out of 10 high-speed TGV trains were operating, along with about two-thirds of regional trains.
The latest mobilization followed several rounds of protests in France since September, initially led by students demanding better educational conditions before spreading to the streets and drawing in other groups.
On Oct 17, around 550,000 people took part in demonstrations across France, bringing together farmers, climate activists, trade unions and students. Their demands covered fuel prices, the cost of living, education and public services.

BELGRADE - Serbian authorities detained nine active police officers and one former officer on Wednesday for allegedly using excessive force against detainees during anti-corruption protests last year in Belgrade, the country's Interior Ministry said.
Anti-corruption protests swept across Serbia, a candidate for European Union membership, in 2025 and 2026 after 16 people died when a concrete canopy on a renovated railway station in the city of Novi Sad collapsed on Nov 1, 2024.
The protests ultimately resulted in the ouster of the populist Serbian Progressive Party (SNS), which is loyal to ex-President Aleksandar Vucic, in an Oct 25 snap parliamentary election by the leaderless Student List.
The ministry said in a statement that the suspects, who served in an elite unit, tortured and intimidated several persons detained in the garage of the government compound in downtown Belgrade on Aug 14-15, 2025.
"By the use of force and threats, (they) inflicted great pain and severe suffering on a larger number of ... persons, intending to obtain information from them and intimidating and unlawfully punishing them," the statement said.
The ministry provided only the initials of the officers and former officer who were detained. The news marked the first major arrests in connection with violence against the protesters who took to the streets over the past two years.
Police used tear gas, crowd-control vehicles and stun grenades to disperse crowds during the protests.
Dozens of students and activists who took part in the protests accused police of excessive use of force that resulted in injuries and psychological trauma.
Serbian students, activists, opposition politicians and rights watchdogs have accused Vucic and the SNS of political violence, pressure against media, ties to organised crime and rampant corruption. Vucic and his allies deny the accusations.

LYON/PARIS - French riot police fired tear gas and charged a group of protesters in the French city of Lyon on Thursday as demonstrations took place across the country in a renewed challenge to President Emmanuel Macron after weeks of unrest.
Some of the protesters were seen shouting anti-police slogans and some were dressed all in black, with face coverings.
The Interior Ministry said police in Lyon had been confronted by several hundred hardcore protesters and that the violence had been contained although tensions remained high.
The nationwide protests and a strike by public workers, called by trade unions and backed by student groups, come after demonstrations over living costs, public services and education triggered the worst civil unrest France has seen in years.
The unrest comes only five months before a presidential election which could pit far-right candidate Marine Le Pen against hard-left rival Jean-Luc Melenchon in a second-round run-off. Macron, who is serving his second term and is unpopular, cannot run again.

"This criminal behaviour is an absolute scandal," said Education Minister Edouard Geffray on X.
French high school students and other demonstrators had earlier faced off against riot police in Paris as they blocked a street with rubbish bins on Thursday morning.
While the demonstrations in Lyon and other cities including Marseille and Nantes began in the morning, the main protest in Paris was only scheduled to start at 2 pm (1300 GMT), from Place de la Republique.
The Eiffel Tower was shut on Thursday because of the strike.
"The government tried to block the demonstrations. The demonstrations are bigger than ever," Sophie Binet, head of the CGT trade union, told reporters. She estimated that some 250,000 people were demonstrating across France.
"High school students, pensioners, students, workers, all together. Unity wins," read one banner at the protest in Lyon.

Authorities had sought to lower tensions after riots and looting on Oct 17 caught security forces off guard, leaving damaged shops and public buildings across the country and fuelling criticism of Macron's handling of the crisis.
Around 70,000 police were on duty nationwide, a comparable figure to recent weekends. The French government has defended police tactics while saying any abuses would be investigated.

The Education Ministry said just over 15 percent of French teaching staff had joined the strike.

Cities across the Czech Republic saw mass protests on Tuesday because of government plans to alter the funding of the country's public broadcasting network.
The government, headed by Prime Minister Andrej Babis, recently introduced a parliamentary bill that seeks to cancel the monthly fee of 205 Czech koruna ($9.48) paid by each household to fund the public broadcasting service, bringing an abrupt end to the debate over the issue, and causing a rushed vote.
Jakub Szanto, a reporter for the Czech TV channel, told Agence-France Presse the attempt to force the bill through without a proper debate was a "radical step", and added "we firmly hope the government will understand that when something's working, you don't have to change it".
In an unprecedented move, workers at public radio and television services joined the protest, with only repeated programs and shorter news bulletins broadcast, and a message on screen saying: "We are doing everything we can to ensure that we can continue to provide a public service in its full scope and quality in the future."
Critics of the government's proposal, which would result in a funding cut of around 15 percent, say it is an attempt to limit the independence of the trusted public broadcasting service.
"This proposal, if passed, will give the government coalition tools to politically influence the content of both public media," said strike committee member Jan Molacek.
Babis, however, insists there is no political agenda and it is purely down to a matter of financing. "None of what the people are protesting against ... is true," he said.
OSLO -- About 40,000 high school students across Denmark walked out of classes Wednesday to protest government plans to cut student grants, organizers said.
The Danish student organization DGS said students at 57 high schools suspended classes, according to Danish news agency Ritzau.
In Copenhagen, protesters gathered at City Hall Square before marching to Christiansborg, the seat of parliament, carrying banners calling for student support to be preserved. DGS and 20 other education and political youth organizations organized the demonstration.
The government's proposal would remove grants for students living with their parents whose combined parental income exceeds a specified threshold, and reduce payments for some others. The proposed cuts form part of its budget negotiations.
Christina Egelund, minister for Science, Higher Education and Digital Affairs, said she understood students demonstrating for their views, but defended the need to make spending choices amid growing demands for defense and security funding. The grant proposal remained under negotiation, she said.
PRAGUE -- Thousands of protesters, including university students and artists, took to the streets in Prague and other cities on Tuesday as Czech public television and radio workers held a one-day strike against a government plan to cut financing and move funding to the state budget.
Workers and demonstrators called the plan an attempt to hurt the broadcasters' independence and viability, and they demanded that the government scrap the planned reform. The strike also led to broadcast curbs.
The strike escalates a fight over the future of the broadcasters that a 2025 Reuters Institute survey found were among the country's most followed and most trusted news sources. The government's bill passed a preliminary vote last month and now awaits further debate in parliament.
Czech Television's 24-hour news broadcast showed a darkened newsroom and moderators dressed in black reading, unusually, from a script.
The government of billionaire Prime Minister Andrej Babis, backed by the far-right Freedom and Direct Democracy (SPD) and eurosceptic Motorists parties, has frequently accused private and public media of bias. The coalition wants to cancel a 205-crown ($9.43) monthly household broadcasting fee — currently used for funding — in its overhaul.
Opposition parties have compared the proposed changes to reforms in Hungary under former Prime Minister Viktor Orban, a Babis ally who built an array of pro-government media during his 16 years in office.
"This proposal, if passed, will give the government coalition tools to politically influence the content of both public media," Czech TV reporter Jan Molacek, a member of the strike committee, said.
The government parties introduced the bill unexpectedly on a Friday last month, curbing debate and forcing a preliminary vote before dawn on the Saturday.
On Tuesday, media workers and supporters marched from the Czech Television headquarters to Prague's Wenceslas Square, where thousands gathered to back their demands.
"I want the media to continue to be free," said student Alena Jechova, 23.
CHANGES POSSIBLE IN FURTHER DEBATE
Critics say the funding shift to the budget would remove the relationship between the media and paying viewers, and funding decisions would become open to political pressure.
A 15 percent financing cut proposed as part of the change would reduce public television's nominal budget to the level of 2008 and radio's to that of 2005. The media have called such cuts unbearable.
Babis reiterated on Monday that the government was merely meeting a promise to lighten the financial burden on the public.
"There is no fight over democracy. Nobody wants to take over the public media or bring them under state control," he said, adding that changes to the bill could be made in further debate.
Molacek said a funding change was not impossible but needed to be based on a thorough debate and backed by legal safeguards against meddling.
PARIS - French high school student protests resumed on Monday on a much smaller scale than in past weeks as pupils returned from a two-week break, with youngsters trying to blockade a few high schools in Paris, Marseille and Rennes, French media reported.
By 0830 GMT some 41 high schools — about 1percent of those nationwide — were blockaded, the Education Ministry said.
Hundreds of France's more than 3,000 high schools were blockaded in the weeks prior to the break. The protests, which began in the Paris suburbs in September, have spread across France and broadened beyond education to focus on the cost of living, drawing in trade unionists and activists and sometimes turning violent.
A wave of so-called "Gen Z" protests has swept across European countries including Germany, Italy, Belgium and Albania, driven by grievances including the cost of living, the poor state of schools and perceived corruption.
Last month French protests degenerated into violent clashes with the police and outright riots in several cities, with shops looted, in a test for President Emmanuel Macron's minority government.
"In terms of blockades — or rather, attempted blockades — the phenomenon is very marginal. We're talking about just a handful of units, a few individual schools," Education Minister Edouard Geffray told radio station France Inter on Monday morning.
He said protests are allowed but preventing access to schools is illegal.
Police officers were present, TV footage showed, though not in full anti-riot gear.
Students want the renovation of aging schools, more teachers, more university places and the end of a university admissions system they see as socially unfair.
Geffray offered last week to cap class sizes at 28, dedicate 4 billion euros ($4.47 billion) to renovating schools, create 15,000 new places for students in higher education and a new admission system by 2028. High school student representatives said the offers were not enough and they would keep protesting.
MADRID - Spanish police removed remaining protesters and tents from Madrid's main square early on Tuesday, officials said, after most of the people demonstrating against soaring rents, home shortages and evictions had already left voluntarily.
The pre-dawn operation ended a five-week encampment in the Puerta del Sol square that had become a symbol of Spain's housing crisis and contributed to Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez's decision to call a snap general election for Nov 29.
The protests were sparked by the eviction of an 87-year-old woman, who later died, with marches called by tenants' unions held across Spain.
Similar camps sprang up in squares in other major Spanish cities, as well as in neighbouring Portugal. The one in Sol grew into a miniature city, with its own named "streets", information stands, cultural activities and food distribution.
Riot police moved in to clear the camp of its remaining 334 occupants at around 3 am (0200 GMT) on Tuesday and made two arrests for disobedience and resisting officers, the office of the central government's representative in Madrid said in a statement.
The operation involved some 400 police, along with firefighters and social services, it said.
Protesters said on their Telegram messaging channel that the clearing of the camp had been violent and that several campers were injured after being beaten and attacked with pepper spray.
The government said in a separate statement that there had been no violence or disturbances.
Reuters footage showed city workers cleaning the square with water hoses, scrubbing graffiti from statues and disposing of the large plastic tarps strung between lamp posts.
On Oct 30, the campers' main representatives said they were dissolving their organising committees as their original demand had been met after the government passed several housing measures.
Instead, they planned to focus on a general strike over housing rights and cost of living called for Nov 11.
According to the government, a large number of the people still sleeping there were homeless or belonged to other vulnerable groups.

The protesters, some wearing masks, protective goggles or Halloween-style outfits, gathered at a square in District 4 of Switzerland's largest city and mingled with partygoers on Saturday night. The protest turned violent as some protesters set fire to rubbish bins, e-bikes and e-scooters at various locations, the statement said.
A car was also set alight, and windows at a public transport stop and several shops were damaged.

Two young Swiss men carrying weapons were arrested when collecting objects at a construction site suspected to be used as projectiles against police, a separate police statement said.
According to police, a demonstration also took place in Basel on Saturday evening. Around 150 people marched through the city center, disrupting traffic. Demonstrators erected barricades and set them on fire, while isolated incidents of property damage were reported along the route.
According to Swiss media reports, the protests in Zurich and Basel were held under the slogan "We've had enough -- in Switzerland too," with demonstrators drawing parallels with youth protests in France and other European countries.
MADRID — Another wave of protests swept across major European cities on Saturday, with demonstrators voicing grievances over issues ranging from living conditions to disputes involving public institutions.
Meanwhile, calls for gatherings on social media prompted police to step up security in several cities.
In France, around 320,000 people took part in a new round of protests on Saturday, including about 70,000 in Paris, according to the Interior Ministry. Organizers put the nationwide turnout at nearly 500,000, including 200,000 in the capital.
Around 70,000 police officers and gendarmes were deployed nationwide. Most marches passed off peacefully, though clashes with police and other disturbances were reported in several cities.
Interior Minister Laurent Nunez told reporters that, according to a preliminary tally, 422 people had been arrested across France.
In Germany, demonstrations took place in more than 30 cities on Saturday.
Protesters called for greater investment in public services and measures to address the rising cost of living, and opposed compulsory military service. By late afternoon, organizers reported more than 68,000 participants, based on figures from 12 cities.
The demonstrations were also largely peaceful, but clashes broke out in several cities in the evening. German news agency dpa reported that police officers were injured in Frankfurt, barricades were set ablaze in Hamburg, and officers came under attack in Bremen.
In Portugal, housing protests drew more than 1,000 people each in Lisbon and Porto on Saturday.
In Lisbon, demonstrators marched toward Rossio Square, where they planned to camp overnight. Police had dispersed protest encampments in both cities on Oct 25.
Political grievances also dominated protests in various countries. In Poland, an escalating dispute over the composition and leadership of the Constitutional Tribunal brought demonstrators onto the streets of Warsaw.
Protesters gathered outside the tribunal from Friday evening into Saturday, with police reporting that four people were detained during overnight operations around the building, local media reported.
Polish President Karol Nawrocki returned early from a foreign trip and announced urgent political consultations.
In Albania, protests demanding better education, improved living conditions and Prime Minister Edi Rama's resignation continued for a 19th consecutive day in Tirana on Saturday, according to the Albanian Telegraphic Agency.
Some protesters threw stones and Molotov cocktails at the police, who responded with tear gas and water cannons. The opposition Democratic Party backed the movement.
Elsewhere, social media calls for gatherings prompted heightened security measures.
In the Netherlands, police dispersed a crowd near The Hague Central Station on Saturday afternoon and urged the public to avoid the city center. Local police reported 34 arrests during the day.
The gathering followed online calls for demonstrations in several Dutch cities, some of which encouraged looting and vandalism. Some businesses boarded up their storefronts ahead of the planned gatherings.
In Spain, police stepped up security in cities including Madrid, Barcelona and Valencia on Saturday following online calls for gatherings, local media reported.
The deployments aimed to prevent a repeat of the previous weekend's unrest in Barcelona, which left 15 officers injured and led to 128 arrests.
Spanish news agency EFE reported on Friday that police had arrested 10 people nationwide on suspicion of inciting unrest and violence through online channels.

Germany has become the latest country in Europe to witness scenes of urban unrest after cost-of-living protests on Saturday turned violent.
Largely peaceful demonstrations organized by a group called Gen Z regelt, meaning "Gen Z takes charge", took place in cities including Hamburg, Cologne, Berlin and the country's financial center, Frankfurt, focusing on issues such as public spending cuts and underfunding in schools, the same issue that was the starting point for major disturbances in France in recent weeks.
A large area of Frankfurt's main shopping district was closed off overnight by police after premises were looted, and across the country there were reports of water cannons being used to disperse people who were pelting emergency workers with missiles.
Roman Poseck, the interior minister of Hesse state, where Frankfurt is situated, said it was "shameful" how the protests had ended, and that "around 100 clearly identified left-wing extremists" had infiltrated them.
"We all have those images from France in our minds — looting, burning houses, burning police vehicles, and attacks on police officers," he said. "France must not be held up as a model in this regard."
Germany's Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt told newspaper Bild that the actions of police had prevented "violence like that seen in Paris", and vowed that troublemakers would receive "harsh punishments".
Gen Z regelt spokesperson Lina Friedrich said "What matters most to us is better support for the health and education systems … we are against compulsory military service and we are in favor of a fairer distribution of the burden."
France also saw more widespread protests on Saturday, with organizers estimating half a million people turned out, and the authorities saying around 320,000, but compared to the destruction witnessed in recent weeks, the latest demonstrations were largely peaceful.
Police had come in for heavy criticism for losing control of previous protests, which degenerated into violence and looting, and prompted French Interior Minister Laurent Nunez to tender his resignation, which was rejected.
"Security forces held their ground and protected city centers," he said on Saturday night. "Public order was maintained."
The tension in France has an added political dimension, as the country begins its build-up to the presidential election taking place in six months' time.
This week, incumbent President Emmanuel Macron, who by law is not allowed to seek a third consecutive term of office, said it was "not normal" for the country to be in such a state of "turmoil" with an election on the horizon.
The current wave of protests emerged from teachers going on strike over school funding, with students coming out in support, and Le Monde newspaper quoted student union spokeswoman Sabrine Kharbach as saying "Starting this Tuesday, we will blockade our universities and high schools, and we will do so again on Thursday before joining the demonstration on Nov 5 called by workers and trade unions".

WARSAW - Protesters clashed with police outside Poland's Constitutional Tribunal in Warsaw on Friday night after the court's president was suspended amid an escalating dispute between Prime Minister Donald Tusk's government and the opposition over control of the judiciary.
Police detained three protesters who entered the court's premises. Several people were reportedly injured, according to local media.
Hundreds of demonstrators, including supporters of the opposition Law and Justice (PiS) party, gathered outside the tribunal, accusing Tusk's government of attempting to seize control of the court. PiS leader Jaroslaw Kaczynski and former Prime Minister Mateusz Morawiecki were among those present.
The confrontation followed months of disputes over judicial appointments. Poland's parliament, where Tusk's ruling coalition holds a majority, has elected eight judges to the tribunal this year. However, President Karol Nawrocki, an ally of the opposition PiS party, accepted the oaths of office of only three.
The remaining five took their oaths at parliamentary ceremonies, but Nawrocki and the tribunal's president refused to recognize their status, preventing them from taking up their judicial duties.
On Friday evening, the tribunal's website announced that its president and vice president had been suspended for one month, with a newly elected judge whose status remains disputed assuming temporary leadership. The tribunal's leadership rejected the move, accusing those involved of illegally taking over its website and describing the action as a "coup."
Nawrocki cut short his trip to Senegal and returned to Poland, where he held an emergency meeting with judicial officials late Friday. Shortly after midnight, he visited the Constitutional Tribunal and entered the court building.
