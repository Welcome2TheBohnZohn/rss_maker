TITLE:
Shooting clouds, sharing dreams

PUBLISHED:
2026-11-28

ARTICLE TEXT:
A fluffy white cloud drifting over Beijing struck one woman as a bear, so she snapped a photo and posted it on a WeChat community for cloud chasers. Another member stared at it and saw a frog instead. A third member then sketched both animals right onto copies of the uploaded photo — bear and frog side by side — sending ripples of laughter across the platform.
An internet company employee known online as Huang Lutu launched the WeChat mini-program Meitianpaiyun, or Snap a Cloud, with two friends in 2019, turning a simple pleasure of photographing clouds into a shared passion for hundreds of thousands of strangers across China.
"The clouds may be the same, but they spark different images in each person's imagination," said Huang, who lives in Beijing and chases clouds whenever she can — on her way to work, during lunch breaks, on coffee runs, or on the way home. "What started as a hobby has now become a habit."
Users of the mini-program share striking images of clouds, identify various cloud formations, and exchange observations. Huang also runs a small chat group for cloud lovers, which now has more than 100 members.
In Guangdong province, summer skies often brim with bright-white clouds set against the blue. Huang had always admired nature's spectacular canvas when she lived in Shenzhen, Guangdong, a few years ago.
She recalled how fellow enthusiast Hu Yongxin once photographed the clouds and posted an image online, explaining the weather phenomenon behind them. The post took off — 200,000 views in a single day and more than 3,000 cloud photos pouring in from people across the country. "That's when I realized how many people love taking pictures of clouds," she said.
In Beijing, the summer of 2026 offered more reasons to look up. Northeast cold vortices — cold-core cyclonic systems that sweep in from northeastern China — brought crisp blue skies and vivid white clouds, triggering a fresh wave of photos online.
Xu Doudou, a primary school teacher in the city, traces her fascination with clouds to her student days in Madrid, Spain, where she said the clouds loomed larger and hung much lower than anything she had seen before. "They were always so beautiful that I had to photograph them."
But she didn't stop at taking pictures. She would imagine what the clouds resembled, and then draw her ideas onto these photos. "I liked letting my imagination run wild," she recalled.
Back in Beijing, Xu found her eyes drifting away from recognizable shapes and toward the colors of the entire sky — the magnificent glow of a sunrise or sunset.
Brought together by their shared love of clouds, members of the group often find themselves chatting about much more than just the sky. "They're like friends who keep each other company, but at a comfortable distance," Huang said.
In August, a group of these cloud enthusiasts met in person and traveled together through Qinghai and Gansu provinces.
"Cloud watching is about observing everyday life — taking the time, amid all the worries, to appreciate the beauty nature offers," said Hu, who was part of the traveling group.
Xu, who also joined the tour, said she remembers how the still waters of Toson Lake reflected the clouds above.
Liu Yuzu, who helped Huang build the mini-program, said that she sees sending a cloud photo as a quiet way of reaching out. "It's like saying, 'I saw something beautiful, I recorded it, and I want you to see it too'," Liu added.
Clouds have inspired Chinese art for centuries, from ancient cloud-and-thunder patterns to flowing ruyi motifs.
Zhou Yue, an associate professor at Tsinghua University's Academy of Arts & Design, noted that in ancient China's agricultural society, watching the clouds also helped people anticipate when it would rain.
Young cloud watchers today are rediscovering the inspiration that shaped traditional Chinese art and culture, Zhou said. "Tradition stays alive not in museum display cases, but in the way people see the world," he added.
