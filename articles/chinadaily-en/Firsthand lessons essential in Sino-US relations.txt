TITLE:
Firsthand lessons essential in Sino-US relations

PUBLISHED:

ARTICLE TEXT:
During his visit to the United States in September, Chinese President Xi Jinping invited 100,000 young Americans to visit China for exchanges and study over the next five years. A similar invitation given in 2023 saw 50,000 young Americans visit China over the following three years.
The 2026 APEC Economic Leaders' Meeting in Shenzhen, South China's Guangdong province, underscored the importance of cooperation in an increasingly divided world. But lasting cooperation requires more than agreements among governments; it also depends on people who can understand different societies and communicate across cultural differences. Educational exchanges, particularly among young people, can help build the mutual understanding and trust needed for this cooperation.
While a large number of Chinese students have gone to the US, relatively fewer Americans have studied in China. According to the Institute of International Education, 1,749 US students studied in China for academic credit in 2023-24, compared with 277,398 Chinese students enrolled in US institutions that year. Although these figures are not directly comparable, they illustrate the imbalance in educational exchange between the two countries.
That landscape is gradually changing. Chinese universities have made substantial advances in research capacity and international engagement. The 2025 Nature Index ranked Zhejiang University, Tsinghua University and Shanghai Jiao Tong University third, fourth and fifth, respectively, in its overall institutional table. China ranked first among countries and territories. While these rankings measure research output in selected scientific journals rather than the overall quality of universities, they demonstrate China's growing research strength.
China is also attracting students from around the world. Official data show that during the 2024-25 academic year, about 380,000 international students from 191 countries and regions studied or participated in exchange programs in China.
To revitalize the educational relationship between China and the US, we must look beyond university campuses.
Both high school students and university students can benefit from visiting China. Stepping outside one's comfort zone, engaging with unfamiliar ideas and reconsidering familiar assumptions can help students become more independent and critical thinkers. Studying abroad provides a richer basis for forming their own judgments.
There is also a distinctive educational value in being a minority. Students studying in a society where their language, cultural references and everyday habits are not the norm must learn to listen carefully, communicate across differences and see themselves through other people's eyes.
They may experience the discomfort of not immediately understanding how things work, but that discomfort can be productive, fostering humility, adaptability and a greater appreciation of diverse perspectives.
These experiences can have lasting professional value. China is the world's second-largest economy and a key player in global manufacturing, trade, technology and scientific research. For US graduates entering international business, technology, academia, journalism or public service, firsthand experience of China can be a differentiating asset in the job market.
Employers working across borders may value candidates who understand not only China's economic importance but also its business practices, consumers, institutions and social context. A person who has studied in China brings something different from someone whose knowledge comes solely from coursework, news coverage or occasional business meetings.
I first visited China in 1981. Since then, I have returned many times, including periods of living and working here and engaging with Chinese universities, businesses and policymakers. The China I saw on my first visit bears little resemblance to today's China. This has reinforced one belief: countries are difficult to understand when viewed only from afar.
The US has benefited greatly from generations of Chinese students who studied at US universities and contributed to research, business and communities there. China has likewise benefited from Chinese students who studied abroad and returned with knowledge, networks and new perspectives.
A larger flow of US students to China would not diminish the appeal of US universities. Nor should US students be expected to return with uniform views of China. But what matters is that their judgments are informed by firsthand experience rather than only from a distance.
The benefit of bringing 100,000 young Americans to China should not be measured simply by the number of visitors. Its greater value lies in creating opportunities for young Americans and Chinese to learn from one another and develop more nuanced perspectives.
A more balanced educational exchange can create something both countries need: more people capable of understanding the other side without necessarily agreeing with it. That is a worthwhile investment, not only in education, but also in the future of China-US relations.
The author is executive vice-chancellor, American president, and distinguished professor of social science at Duke Kunshan University in China.
