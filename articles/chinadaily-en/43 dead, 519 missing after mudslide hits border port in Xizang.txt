China on Monday said it had invited foreign journalists to visit the mudslide-hit areas of Gyirong in the Xizang autonomous region to learn about rescue efforts and reconstruction progress in response to the international media's close attention to reporting on the debris flow disaster.
The visit came after round-the-clock repair work had initially reopened the roads to the disaster zone, Foreign Ministry spokeswoman Mao Ning said at a regular news briefing.
China had invited journalists from eight foreign media outlets, including NBC, CNN, Reuters, RT and the Press Trust of India, to enter the hardest-hit areas to learn about rescue and reconstruction progress, Mao said. They also attended a news conference held by the regional government, she added.
As reports from these journalists have been released, Mao said, "China will continue to make every effort to carry out search-and-rescue operations and handling of the aftermath, and release the latest information on the disaster and rescue operations in a timely manner."
After days of relentless search and rescue efforts, Xiao Fugui, a police officer at the entry-exit border inspection station in Gyirong county, Shigatse, Xizang autonomous region, looked gaunt and exhausted.
Despite his weary appearance, he repeatedly insisted that he was not at all tired. "All I want is to find my workmates," he said on Thursday, gripping an iron shovel with determination, his brow furrowed.
On Aug 26, a massive mudslide devastated Gyirong Port, a vital land link between China and Nepal. As of 6 pm on Saturday, 993 personal belongings had been recovered while 519 people remain unaccounted for.
Xiao was driving toward the port when the disaster struck. "I was just 4 or 5 kilometers away when I saw several cars pulled over," he said. "I got out and asked other drivers what was happening — everyone was panicked, saying the water level had risen sharply near the port."
Concerned for their safety, he instructed the two female colleagues in his car to get out and find a safe place, then continued driving alone to assess the situation. Upon realizing it was a mudslide, Xiao immediately alerted authorities.
"The muddy water was rushing toward me, so I threw the car into reverse and backed up about 500 meters before stopping," said Xiao in a tense voice. "I was terrified the car would be swept away. What really scared me was not knowing what had happened to my workmates at the port."
As visibility dropped sharply and the road ahead was blocked, Xiao found himself trapped. Reflecting on that moment, he said, "I really walked through the gates of hell that day."
Driven by worry and a deep sense of duty, Xiao joined the rescue teams. By Sunday, he had reached the vicinity of the port and immediately immersed himself in the relief efforts. "As soon as I arrived, I started moving supplies, then began searching. I found clothing, shoes and crushed vehicles belonging to the missing. Every single item I saw pierced my heart like a needle."
Originally from Qinghai province, 39-year-old Xiao has worked in Xizang for 17 years, with 11 of those years at the Gyirong entry-exit border inspection station, primarily handling publicity affairs. "For many years, I've spent almost every day with my workmates — filming their patrols, writing about their lives guarding this border," he said. "This port, these people — they've become part of me."
On the first day of the rescue, Xiao and other workers camped in a tent by a reservoir halfway up the mountain near the border port. He did not sleep at all. "The rain was so heavy that night — water poured down the slopes and merged into the river. The sound of rain, rushing water and collapsing hillsides all mixed together. It made my heart sink. I didn't dare to sleep, and I couldn't sleep anyway," he said.
As he spoke, he picked up the shovel and resumed digging. "I have only one thought: no matter what, I want to find my workmates. I want to bring them home," he added.

As dawn broke in Gyirong county following another night of continuous rain on Saturday, He Lei, a psychological counselor, began another day of providing counseling services to search and rescue personnel working on the front line of the mudslide-stricken Gyirong Port in Shigatse, Xizang autonomous region.
"Through our work these days, we have found that our soldiers are quite resilient. They are focused on fulfilling their missions here and are fully aware of the significance of helping every family affected by the disaster," said He, a member of the medical and disease prevention team with the logistics support department of the People's Liberation Army Xizang Military Command.
As of Thursday, the PLA and the People's Armed Police Force had deployed 726 personnel to support rescue and relief operations following the mudslide, according to official data.
Li Guokai, head of the medical and disease prevention team, said the team is responsible for providing medical and psychological services to rescue workers and affected villagers during patrol visits, disinfecting key sites and monitoring health risks.
According to He, for those experiencing stress responses such as panic attacks or physical stiffness, the most important thing is to divert their attention from whatever is triggering the reaction.
"We have a method called 'look, listen, smell and move.' For instance, we will guide soldiers in need to identify three objects with specific characteristics in their surroundings, then listen for distinct sounds, identify several different smells and engage in a simple physical movement, such as stamping their feet," she said.
"This rapid redirection of attention helps alleviate immediate physiological stress responses," she said.
In addition to patrolling military bases involved in rescue operations on a daily basis, He also goes to the core area to provide on-site assistance when needed.
"As members of the military, we cannot shy away from difficult conditions," she said. "I wanted to be on the front line myself — to see if any soldiers needed support and to teach them techniques they can use on their own to manage stress."
Tan Xiangdong, Party chief of the Xizang autonomous regional health commission, said at a news conference on Wednesday that national and regional health authorities had organized three batches of psychological health experts, totaling more than 210.
Frontline rescuers have access to psychological risk assessments, group and individual counseling, as well as pocket guides designed to help them understand stress reactions and learn effective ways to cope, Tan said.

For Hao Zhen, who was among a network of volunteers coordinating relief efforts in disaster-hit Gyirong county, rendering aid can come in many forms.
When a devastating mudslide struck Gyirong, a remote border area in the Xizang autonomous region, Hao did not hesitate.
While professional rescue teams searched the hardest-hit areas, Hao and his fellow volunteers worked behind the scenes to distribute supplies, meals and arrange accommodation for displaced residents and rescue workers.
The 27-year-old was among 17 volunteers from China's Go West program who joined relief efforts in Gyirong township after the Aug 26 disaster.
"When the disaster first happened, a lot of affected residents and rescuers arrived in the area all at once," Hao said. "The first thing we had to do was make sure they had somewhere to stay and food to eat."
As relief supplies poured in, the volunteers counted, sorted and moved thousands of boxes donated by a number of Red Cross organizations, companies and foundations. They also helped with logistics at the local emergency response headquarters and collected and organized information from the disaster area.
The experience gave Hao a new understanding of the mechanics of disaster relief efforts.
"Before this, I had only read news stories about volunteers helping after disasters," he said. "Now I realize that you don't have to be on the search-and-rescue front line to make a difference. Keeping things running behind the scenes matters, too."
Born in 1999, Hao was the oldest among the volunteers working in Gyirong who came from various parts of the country, including Heilongjiang, Jiangxi and Shandong provinces, as well as Xizang, and have backgrounds ranging from medicine to education.
The work brought practical challenges. Only two of the 17 volunteers spoke Tibetan, creating communication difficulties at shelters. Local township officials often stepped in to assist with interpretations.
Food and accommodation were also stretched in the first few days, as large numbers of residents, rescuers and support workers arrived in the small border town. Hao said the situation gradually improved as local authorities coordinated with hotels and restaurants to provide additional support.
Five other Go West program volunteers remained in the county seat, where they welcomed relatives of missing people, assisted families and handled calls and follow-ups through the 12345 public service hotline.
Volunteers from nearby counties, including Lhaze and Dingri, also arrived to help.
For Hao, Gyirong is a long way from home — nearly 5,700 kilometers from Shuangyashan in Heilongjiang. He usually makes the journey home once a year.
He arrived in Gyirong in February 2025 to join the Go West program and planned to stay for three years. His family initially tried to talk him out of the move, worried about the distance and the physical demands of living at high altitude.
"But I thought, if everyone worries about the hardship and the distance, places like this will have fewer and fewer young people willing to come," Hao said.
The physical challenges are real. When Hao first arrived in Gyirong, he found that he did not have the same stamina he had at lower elevations. He also experienced heart palpitations when he first joined the relief effort. After more than a year on the plateau, however, he said his body has largely adapted.
His family still worries about him, particularly after learning that he was involved in the disaster response.
"But the disaster happened right here, where we work and live," Hao said. "Since we chose to come here as volunteers, helping when something like this happens is simply part of what we should do."

For Hao, the experience has also changed the way he approached the small tasks that fill much of his day.
Moving boxes, arranging accommodation and making sure people have meals may be far removed from the dramatic scenes of a search-and-rescue operation, but he no longer sees them as minor jobs.
"My biggest hope now is that we can keep the support work running smoothly, that all the rescuers return safely and that there are no more secondary disasters," he said.

China has established communication and information-sharing mechanisms with the 23 home countries of the 261 foreign nationals who remain missing following the mudslide that struck Gyirong Port on the China-Nepal border.
Through the China-Nepal disaster information exchange mechanism, meteorological, hydrological, glacial lake and glacier data are being transmitted to Nepal daily, a senior official said on Sunday.
Ren Wei, executive deputy commander of the emergency rescue headquarters and executive vice-chairman of the Xizang autonomous region, said all relevant information had been promptly communicated to the respective embassies in China through diplomatic channels.
A 24-hour dedicated hotline for families of the missing foreign nationals has also been established and has received 509 calls as of Sunday. Each inquiry had been followed up on, with responses relayed to the next of kin, Ren said.
At the request of the Nepali side, the Chinese government dispatched tunnel rescue and DNA identification experts to Nepal, along with emergency relief supplies. The Xizang autonomous regional government also provided 320 metric tons of Bailey bridge components — designed for the rapid assembly of temporary bridges — and other relief supplies to Nepal.
Yang Lahong, deputy director of the Xizang foreign affairs office, said the region had carried out verification of information on the missing people in a rigorous, prudent and standardized manner.
The region released basic information on the missing people initially identified a day after the disaster. On Aug 30, it published additional details of the missing foreign nationals verified through joint investigations and rapid cross-checking by multiple departments.
While the breakdown by country and number of missing people was made public by authorities, the specific list and personal details have not been released due to privacy concerns. Instead, the information was communicated to the relevant embassies through diplomatic channels, with a collective briefing held on Tuesday, Yang said.
For safety reasons and to ensure efficient and orderly rescue work, no non-professionals were allowed to enter the affected core areas until conditions were fully prepared, she said.
To keep the public informed while ensuring safety, a small number of Chinese journalists were arranged to accompany rescue teams into the core areas despite the difficult terrain. Their reports and footage were subsequently released and widely used by domestic and international media, Yang said.
Regarding the families of the missing foreign nationals, the region expressed sympathy during these difficult times. In addition to providing regular authoritative updates and notifying families through diplomatic channels, authorities have kept the 24-hour hotline operating, with personnel diligently verifying information and providing feedback.
Currently, efforts are focused on rescue and recovery. After the search operation concludes, authorities will confirm and notify the status of deceased foreign nationals and assist their families with follow-up procedures.
For those who remain missing, assistance will be provided within China's legal framework, including arrangements for their families to visit Xizang, in coordination with relevant foreign diplomatic missions, she added.

Chinese experts have intensified monitoring of landslides, barrier lakes, glaciers and glacial lakes around the mudslide-hit area in Gyirong county, Xizang autonomous region, to guard against secondary disasters and ensure the safety of ongoing rescue operations.
The mudslide, which occurred on Aug 26, caused significant casualties and left many people missing in the region.
Su Pengcheng, a researcher at the Institute of Mountain Hazards and Environment under the Chinese Academy of Sciences, said during a group interview with Chinese and foreign media in Gyirong on Sunday that experts from multiple government departments and research institutions had been mobilized soon after the disaster.
While rescue operations were underway, specialists began analyzing the causes of the disaster and assessing potential geological risks to provide safety support for frontline rescuers, Su said.
The road leading to the core rescue area was reopened in less than a week, while monitoring and risk assessment continued in parallel.
One priority has been closely tracking changes in a barrier lake formed after the disaster to prevent any sudden discharge from threatening downstream areas.
Authorities have also deployed professional monitoring equipment, including remote slope-deformation cameras, slope radars and satellite remote-sensing systems, to continuously assess the stability of slopes and residual deposits in the disaster-hit area.
Particular attention has been paid to potentially unstable material remaining on slopes near the affected valley, which could trigger further landslides or debris flows and pose risks to downstream rescue operations, Su said.
Experts have also conducted comprehensive geological hazard surveys around five villages in the affected river basin and along National Highway G216, a key emergency rescue route, to identify potential dangers and ensure the route remains safe.
Monitoring has extended beyond the core disaster area, with satellite and aerial remote sensing being used to track changes in surrounding glaciers and glacial lakes, which are widely distributed across the high-altitude region.
Su said the operation has brought together expertise from emergency management, natural resources, meteorology and water resources authorities, as well as research institutions, with a range of monitoring equipment deployed.
Experts are also using real-time images transmitted by drones to assess changing conditions. Their findings are promptly relayed to downstream teams, allowing search and rescue personnel to adjust operations when risks emerge.
Su said the coordinated monitoring system is intended to provide continuous early warning of possible secondary geological disasters while rescue work continues.
The recent debris flow that occurred on the Nepali side has underscored the urgent need for cross-border cooperation in disaster early warning, said Norbu Tsering, acting mayor of Shigatse, on Sunday.
Su Pengcheng, a researcher at the Institute of Mountain Hazards and Environment, Chinese Academy of Sciences, said that source zones of ice-rock avalanches at high altitudes are extremely difficult to access because of cold temperatures, low oxygen levels and rugged terrain.
Cross-border river basins also have observational blind spots, while ground equipment can suffer from power and communication problems. Precursor signals are often subtle, and warning windows can be only a matter of minutes — far too short for traditional single-point monitoring to cover entire watersheds, he said.
Given that climate change is accelerating such rare but severe events, it is essential to conduct risk assessments of glacier instability, fill upstream monitoring gaps and introduce new geophysical detection technologies for full-chain dynamic monitoring, Su said.
He proposed developing a "space-air-ground" noncontact monitoring network to reduce dependence on on-site stations at extreme altitudes. A tiered approach — involving wide-area satellite scanning, targeted high-resolution monitoring and downstream alert systems — could provide valuable additional time for evacuation, he said.
Su also called for cross-border scientific collaboration and joint early-warning mechanisms. Since disasters do not respect borders, he called for data exchanges and shared risk assessments with Himalayan nations through existing international platforms. He also urged the creation of rapid notification channels to extend early warnings across entire upstream-downstream basins.
Norbu said the Xizang autonomous region has invested heavily in glacial-lake monitoring in recent years. A baseline survey using satellite imagery, fieldwork and glacier data identified more than 33,000 glacial lakes across the region.
Authorities have placed 187 high-risk lakes under satellite monitoring, with 49 of those also covered by ground sensors and drone surveillance, he said.
A multi-agency data-sharing platform now links meteorological, hydrological, natural resources and emergency departments. Expert panels regularly review glacial-lake risks, and a short-term early-warning system has been activated, along with evacuation drills for downstream communities during the flood season, he said.

China has deployed around 20 sets of monitoring equipment across the disaster-hit Gyirong area in the Xizang autonomous region, with real-time data showing geological conditions stabilizing, a senior geologist said.
The devices — including debris flow monitors, slope radars and microseismic sensors — were installed by the China Geological Survey and other emergency response teams following last month's ice-rock avalanche that buried parts of the Gyirong border crossing, causing massive casualties on both sides of the border.
Ge Daqing, deputy director of the China Aero Geophysical Survey and Remote Sensing Center for Natural Resources, said the equipment was positioned at key risk points across the disaster zone to monitor slope movement, debris flow channels and ground deformation in real time.
Two additional slope radars were installed on Wednesday near the border crossing after rescue teams cleared access roads, he added.
The devices are positioned to protect workers repairing roads and power lines by issuing early warnings if falling rocks or shallow landslides are detected on the slopes above.
Data collected so far indicates the dynamic processes at the site have entered a relatively stable phase. "Overall, there have been no significant fluctuations or changes," Ge said, adding that the risk of a large-scale secondary disaster is currently assessed as low without external triggers. However, he cautioned that high-risk factors persist due to the complex terrain.
Some minor rockfalls have been observed at the glacier front in the collapse zone, but Ge said these pose no immediate threat.
The monitoring equipment is primarily intended for the emergency response phase. Once a comprehensive risk assessment is completed and no major threats remain, the devices may be withdrawn, he said.
Beyond the immediate response, the disaster has underscored the limitations of existing monitoring technologies in the high-altitude, cloud-prone Himalayan region.
Ge said optical satellite imagery — the most common remote sensing tool — is severely hampered by persistent cloud cover and fog in the area. Clear images can be obtained on only about 20 days a year, and most of the optical data received recently have been obscured by clouds.
To address this, Ge called for the accelerated development of new synthetic aperture radar satellites, particularly an interferometric SAR satellite constellation that combines L-band, high-resolution, wide-swath capabilities, and both sun-synchronous and low-inclination orbits. This can overcome the challenge of monitoring rapid deformation in the high-elevation mountains and glaciers of the Himalayas.
He also stressed the need to improve satellite revisit cycles and spatial resolution to enable more frequent and rapid monitoring.
Unmanned aerial vehicles offer another monitoring option, but current drones struggle to operate above 7,000 meters and their sensors remain vulnerable to cloud interference. New radar-based sensors and improved high-altitude flight capabilities are needed, he added.
Looking ahead, Ge warned that the risk of high-altitude ice-rock avalanches in the region is likely to increase.
"The 2015 Nepal earthquake caused internal damage to these mountain masses," he said.
"Combined with glacial erosion at high elevations and steep terrain, the structural integrity of rock formations has been compromised, making them prone to collapse."
Given the transboundary nature of such hazards, Ge highlighted the importance of international collaboration in disaster monitoring and early warning across the Himalayan region, noting that ice-rock avalanches and glacial lake outburst floods do not respect national borders.

The death toll from the massive mudslide that struck a border port in Gyirong county, Shigatse, Xizang autonomous region, on Aug 26, rose to 31 as of 6 pm on Friday, while 531 people remained missing. A total of 987 personal items had also been recovered, according to the disaster emergency and rescue command headquarters.
The rescue access route to Gyirong Port has been opened, marking a new stage in relief efforts, the headquarters said at a news conference on Friday evening.
The command center said coordinated rescue operations would be further intensified, with particular emphasis on monitoring and early warning for secondary disasters at the port site and along the G216 highway.
Local authorities will also continue to assess risks and issue early warnings for areas upstream and downstream of the disaster site to ensure the safety of rescue operations, the headquarters said.
Since the mudslide hit Gyirong Port, an important land gateway between China and Nepal, round-the-clock rescue operations involving multiple government departments have been underway.
As of 4 pm on Wednesday, 172 experts and technicians from meteorological, natural resources, water resources and emergency management departments, along with 150 sets of equipment, had been deployed to the site, according to the headquarters.
The water resources authority, for example, sent 28 experts with 31 sets of equipment, including drones, radar water-level gauges and high-definition cameras, to conduct hydrological monitoring. The natural resources department dispatched 92 technicians with 40 sets of equipment to carry out focused surveys along two rescue access routes and in areas affected by the mudslide.
The regional government has also moved swiftly to assess geological hazards in the disaster area. As of Wednesday, a special inspection along the G216 highway, a key rescue access route, had identified 58 geological hazard sites. Seven were classified as high-risk, 39 as medium-risk and 12 as low-risk, according to the headquarters.
Local authorities have completed hazard inspections and risk assessments in three affected villages and identified two sites for temporary shelters, while providing guidance on their construction, the headquarters said.
Technology has played a key role in the rescue operation. Slope radar, high-definition video surveillance and geophone monitoring systems have been installed in areas affected by ice-avalanche debris flows to continuously detect anomalies, including ice-rock collapses and deformation of debris in the source zone.
By automatically collecting deformation and image data, the systems help address the limitations of manual inspections in the steep, canyon-locked terrain. They enable real-time monitoring of key hazard sites and help ensure the safety of rescue personnel working downstream, the command center said.
In addition, local authorities have established a regular coordination mechanism to ensure smooth information flow throughout the rescue operation.
Medical and epidemic prevention personnel from the Chinese military have begun testing soil samples from the core area of the mudslide-hit Gyirong Port in Shigatse, Xizang autonomous region, to help formulate disinfection and personal protection plans as detailed search and rescue efforts continue.
Bacterial culture and pathogen testing are being used to gauge the risk of infectious diseases for rescue crews working amid shattered rocks and thick sludge in the core area, said a medical and disease prevention team with the People's Liberation Army Xizang Military Command's logistics support department.
The road access was largely restored on Wednesday morning.
Lhamo Tsering, a military technician specializing in infectious disease prevention and control, said that soil samples collected from the core zone would go through testing for 21 vector-borne pathogens, 23 respiratory pathogens and 32 gastrointestinal pathogens.
"Conducting pathogen testing on these soil samples can identify the types and levels of soil pathogen contamination, assess the risk of secondary infectious diseases, trace the sources of pollution, and guide environmental disinfection and personnel protection," she said.
"The results can also help us evaluate the effectiveness of epidemic control efforts and prevent the spread of soil-borne infectious diseases after a disaster," she added.
Liu Xiaonan, a member of the team, was among the first to enter the core zone on Wednesday after the road leading to the port reopened. He recalled heavy downpours that evening, and his team worked until late before returning.
One of the most challenging parts of his work was navigating the deep mud in the area. "In some areas, the sludge looks solid, but when I try walking on it, my feet can get sucked down until the mud comes up to my calves or even my knees. Sometimes we are only able to pull ourselves out with the help of nearby personnel," he said.
It is also important to prevent mosquito and other insect bites in the area, he added. Lhamo Tsering was one of the two female military personnel working on-site on Friday morning. The other was tasked with providing psychological consulting for search and rescue crew members.
Lhamo Tsering said she had participated in responding to acute health emergencies, including the COVID-19 outbreak, in the past. It was her first time working on the front line in the aftermath of a devastating natural disaster of this scale.
"I am used to performing tests in the laboratory using samples collected and transported back by my fellow soldiers. This time, I asked to come to the hardest-hit area because I wanted to collect the samples myself to ensure the precision of my work," she said.
As a native of Lhasa, the regional capital of Xizang, Lhamo Tsering said she was also deeply saddened by the havoc wreaked by the mudslide at Gyirong Port. "This area is naturally beautiful with pristine air, a comfortable altitude and lush vegetation," she said. "I hope that one day it can restore its beauty."
Despite his weary appearance, he insisted repeatedly that he was not tired at all. "All I want is to find my workmates," he said on Thursday, gripping an iron shovel with determination, his brow furrowed.
On Aug 26, a massive mudslide devastated Gyirong Port, a vital land link between China and Nepal. By Wednesday noon, 21 victims had been found, 847 personal belongings recovered, and 541 people remained missing.
Xiao was driving toward the port when the disaster struck. "I was just four or five kilometers away when I saw several cars pulled over," he said. "I got out and asked other drivers what was happening — everyone was panicked, saying the water level had risen sharply near the port."
Concerned for their safety, he instructed the two female colleagues in his car to get out and find a safe place, then continued driving alone to assess the situation. Upon realizing it was a landslide, Xiao immediately alerted the authorities.
"The muddy water was rushing toward me, so I threw the car into reverse and backed up about 500 meters before stopping," he recounted, his voice tense. "I was terrified the car would be swept away. What really scared me was not knowing what had happened to my workmates at the port."
Originally from Qinghai province, the 39-year-old Xiao has worked in Xizang for 17 years, with 11 of those years at the Gyirong entry-exit border inspection station, primarily handling publicity affairs. "For many years, I've spent almost every day with my workmates — filming their patrols, writing about their lives guarding this border," he said. "This port, these people — they've become part of me."
On the first day of the rescue, Xiao and other workers camped in a tent by a reservoir halfway up the mountain near the border port. He did not sleep at all. "The rain was so heavy that night — water poured down the slopes and merged into the river. The sound of rain, rushing water, and collapsing hillsides all mixed together. It made my heart sink. I didn't dare to sleep, and I couldn't sleep anyway," he said.
