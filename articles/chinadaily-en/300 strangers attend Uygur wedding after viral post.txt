300 strangers attend Uygur wedding after viral post
Earlier this month, a Uygur wedding in Yarkant county of Kashgar prefecture, Xinjiang Uygur autonomous region, became an unexpected nationwide celebration. More than 300 strangers traveled from across the country to share in the joy and blessings of the occasion. The scene that followed, brimming with music and dance, cheer and laughter, was later captured in online posts and videos that spread the festive vibes even farther and wider.
The story began when the groom's mother Zoram Seyit posted a digital invitation on the short-video sharing platform Douyin on Aug 3, expecting only family and friends to respond. But when a curious user from Central China's Henan province asked if they could attend and the host family replied with a simple "yes", the post went viral overnight.
On Aug 8, the wedding day, the banquet hall was filled with guests from diverse regions and ethnic backgrounds, all gathered for the same celebration. Men and women, many in their finest attire, sang and danced throughout the day.
Groom Akramjan Omar said his online guests joined in every part of the celebration, from the midday procession to escort the bride to the wedding hall and the main ceremony in the afternoon, to the after-party that stretched past 9 pm.
He had been unaware of his mother's post until the next morning, when he woke up to find it had already racked up over 10,000 likes. For a family that has run a Xinjiang cuisine restaurant in Henan for nearly three decades, more guests meant more joy.
However, as the numbers kept climbing — likes, comments and promises to attend — the initial excitement gave way to a practical concern: how to take good care of these guests, especially when they had no way of knowing how many would actually show up.
The family then trimmed their original guest list from 500 relatives and friends on each of the bride's and groom's sides to 200 per side and reserved the second floor, which offered a full view of the hall, for strangers traveling from afar. Food supplies were doubled, featuring local specialties such as pilaf, baked stuffed buns, seasonal and dried fruits, as well as wedding sweets, while a Mandarin-speaking partner was added alongside the Uygur-language master of ceremonies.
Many guests attempted to hand over their cash gifts, following Chinese wedding customs, but the host family declined most of them. Akramjan Omar said he was touched at one point seeing people who clearly didn't really know how to dance moving anyway, their faces lit up with pure enjoyment. At the end of the night, the newlyweds and their unexpected guests gathered for a group photo.
Warm hospitality
Wei Lixi, 20, from South China's Guangxi Zhuang autonomous region, was on a solo road trip in Kashgar city when he saw the invitation on Douyin. He had his vehicle washed for the occasion and joined the groom's procession early that morning. The groom led everyone in a dance, and guests were handed party poppers to set off during the liveliest moments.
At first, Wei worried about intruding — but the hosts welcomed the out-of-town visitors with open arms. "The air was electric. I felt like part of one big family," he said.
Hao Shuhui, 24, from Henan, married into a Xinjiang family earlier this year. She dug through her wardrobe for a bright red dress and made her way to the Uygur family that has longstanding ties with her hometown.
The hosts were warm enough to ease her shyness. A groomsman had set up a WeChat group in advance, sharing a few do's and don'ts and encouraging the guests to speak up about any needs. The netizens, meanwhile, were worried about causing trouble.
A guest in his 30s surnamed Tang from Shandong province said, "It was incredibly infectious, even for those of us Han people who aren't particularly good at singing or dancing."
He added that he had immersed himself in the warmth, hospitality and optimism of the Uygur friends, and felt a genuine bond between people of different ethnic backgrounds. Guests like him, too, began chatting and sharing stories of their travels and lives.
Emerging tourism draw
Grand and lively Uygur weddings have long attracted visitors and sparked fascination across China.
In May 2025, the variety show HAHAHAHAHA re-created a ceremony through role-play.
On Aug 12, the Uygur wedding of celebrity couple Madina Memet and Jiang Chao sparked heated online discussions. Some Douyin posts generated several hundred thousand likes, with the most popular exceeding 2 million.
A typical Uygur wedding unfolds with a morning session hosted by the bride's family, followed by the bride-pickup and a transitional segment for the younger crowd that features dancing, the bouquet toss and photo sessions, as well as a banquet by the groom's side with a formal veil-lifting ceremony. Singing and dancing accompany every stage of the wedding. Days before, a "consultation tea party" allows both families to get acquainted and exchange betrothal gifts, while days after, close family members and friends will gather at the bride's home for a visit.
The invitation post for the Yarkant wedding has to date accumulated more than 10 million views and some 77,000 comments. Some netizens posted their own wedding invitations on Douyin, while others simply asked: "Can I come?"
Experiencing a Uygur wedding has gradually become a tourism draw in Xinjiang. Some banquet venues in Urumqi and Kashgar offer viewing areas on the second-floor mezzanines for tourists to watch real ceremonies live. In Kashgar city, 26-year-old visitor Zhang Yao observed a wedding this way. "The women wore an abundance of ornate jewelry. Their dresses had a bold, vivid theme color but were different in design, all absolutely stunning," she said, while also noting the importance of respecting the hosts on their big day.
Yao Jiahui tried her luck on Thursday and happened upon a wedding. A bridesmaid took her along on the dance floor. Aunties asked her in Uygur if she was enjoying the event. "It's all in the dance," she said.
