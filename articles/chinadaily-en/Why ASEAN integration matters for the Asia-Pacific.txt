TITLE:
Why ASEAN integration matters for the Asia-Pacific

PUBLISHED:

ARTICLE TEXT:
As Asia-Pacific leaders prepare to gather in Shenzhen, Guangdong province, for the 33rd Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation Economic Leaders' Meeting on Nov 18-19, they face a region that is more economically integrated than ever, yet increasingly vulnerable to fragmentation. While businesses depend on cross-border production networks, governments continue to impose trade restrictions and measures to protect domestic industries. This tension between economic interconnectedness and policy fragmentation has become a challenge for regional cooperation.
China's APEC 2026 theme, "Building an Asia-Pacific Community to Prosper Together", emphasizes openness, innovation and cooperation — priorities that are both timely and crucial.
The State of Southeast Asia 2026 Survey by the ISEAS-Yusof Ishak Institute also reflects these priorities. When asked how the Association of Southeast Asian Nations should strengthen its resilience and relevance, 28.5 percent of the respondents favored increasing intra-regional trade and investment, while 20.7 percent supported diversifying supply chains to cushion economic tensions. In the face of rising protectionism and nationalism, the largest group of respondents favored accelerating regional integration.
These findings suggest that ASEAN's preferred strategy against growing economic uncertainty is to strengthen internal economic ties while maintaining open partnerships beyond the region. Greater integration can create more opportunities within ASEAN, fortify supply chains, and make the region less vulnerable to external shocks.
The survey also reveals a less discussed problem: 30.2 percent of the respondents identified limited domestic technocratic capacity to implement regional initiatives and 30.1 percent felt development disparities among member states were the leading impediments to intra-regional economic cooperation. Geopolitical pressures, while important, ranked behind these internal constraints.
ASEAN has adopted numerous economic agreements and initiatives, but their effectiveness hinges on national-level implementation. Differences in regulatory capacity, infrastructure and digital readiness can hinder progress.
The question is not whether ASEAN has sufficient instruments, but whether businesses, particularly micro, small and medium-sized enterprises, can practically benefit from these agreements.
Singapore's 2027 ASEAN Chairmanship is an opportunity to advance this agenda. Singapore has prioritized deeper integration and stronger external partnerships. Achieving these goals would help ASEAN attract investment, expand partnerships beyond the region and strengthen its role in regional supply chains.
To advance regional integration, ASEAN must address practical barriers to cross-border trade, including cumbersome customs procedures, inefficient logistics and differences in regulatory criteria. Greater technical cooperation and capability enhancement would also help less-developed member states implement regional commitments, enabling businesses, particularly MSMEs, to benefit more fully from regional integration.
These efforts matter even more as companies adjust their supply chains to manage geopolitical and economic risks. China's manufacturing and technological strengths complement ASEAN's growing markets and production bases, alongside Singapore's role in logistics and financial services. The challenge is to maintain these economic ties while allowing businesses to diversify production and sourcing — without creating new trade barriers or dividing the region into competing economic blocs.
Beyond trade in goods, digital integration is increasingly important. ASEAN's Digital Economy Framework Agreement, whose negotiations concluded in 2026, aims to facilitate digital trade and improve interoperability. If implemented effectively, it could reduce transaction costs and help smaller companies access regional markets through electronic documentation, digital payments and more compatible systems. The challenge will be to ensure that differences in national regulations and digital capabilities do not create new barriers.
APEC plays a different but complementary role. While ASEAN advances integration through regional commitments, APEC brings together a broader group of economies for voluntary cooperation. It can promote trade facilitation, supply-chain connectivity, and compatible digital standards, even when members disagree on broader trade policy. The goal is not to establish identical rules, but to reduce unnecessary friction across markets.
As the current APEC host and a major economic partner of ASEAN, China has a significant role to play. Economic cooperation will be judged by the sustainability and mutual benefits it delivers.
The wider rules-based multilateral trading system, centered on the World Trade Organization, remains just as important. Smaller economies, in particular, rely on predictable rules rather than bargaining power alone. Regional arrangements should reinforce this system, not replace it. Although WTO reform remains difficult, Asia-Pacific economies share an interest in preserving common trade rules and preventing further fragmentation.
Stronger ASEAN integration therefore matters beyond Southeast Asia. It can make regional production networks more resilient, sustain open partnerships and support wider Asia-Pacific cooperation.
In a world of increasing protectionism, the priority should be to make existing frameworks deliver more effectively for businesses and communities, while preserving the common rules which support regional prosperity.
