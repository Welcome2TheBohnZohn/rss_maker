Not quite as smooth as silk

ARTICLE TEXT:
The history of Chinese silk is, in many ways, a history of origins continually pushed further back in time. For decades, the earliest evidence of silk production seemed to lie in Neolithic textile fragments unearthed at sites such as Qianshanyang in Zhejiang province and Qingtai in Henan province, dating to roughly 6,000-4,000 years ago. Earlier still was the half-carbonized silkworm cocoon discovered in Xiyin village in Shanxi province in 1926, bearing traces of cutting that date to the Yangshao Culture period.
That timeline shifted again in 2016. In a study published in the science journal Nature, researchers reported silk protein residues in soil samples from burials at the Jiahu site in Henan, dating back as far as 8,500 years. Along with weaving tools and bone needles unearthed there, the findings suggest that some of China's earliest Neolithic communities may already have possessed a basic knowledge of weaving and the deliberate use of silk fibers.
The conclusion remains debated and awaits further material confirmation. Even so, the broader pattern is unmistakable: with each archaeological discovery, the beginnings of Chinese silk appear to recede ever deeper into prehistory.
"That was an age when humans still lived in profound awe of the forces of nature — a world shaped by ritual, worship, and boundless imaginings about life and death," said Zhao Feng, a leading scholar of Chinese silk history and former director of the China National Silk Museum in Hangzhou, Zhejiang province.
That sense of awe still lingers in the objects left behind. Long before silk became a commodity, the silkworm itself appears to have occupied a place between nature, ritual, and myth.
At the Shuanghuaishu site in Henan, archaeologists unearthed a 5,300-year-old ivory silkworm carved from a wild boar tusk. Depicting a silkworm spinning silk, the object is believed to have been associated with ritual practice or totemic worship, suggesting the silkworm had already acquired sacred significance in prehistoric China. Together with early silk remains found across the central Yellow River region, it points not only to the emergence of sericulture but also to a symbolic world built around silk.
That world deepened over time. In ancient Chinese thought, the silkworm's life cycle — withdrawal into the cocoon, followed by transformation into a moth — came to symbolize metamorphosis, transcendence, and rebirth. A striking example survives in a jade artifact excavated from the Guo State cemetery in Henan, dating from the ninth to seventh centuries BC. Combining the body of a silkworm with the head of a dragon, it reveals how the silkworm entered the realm of cosmological imagination. Jade and gold silkworms were often buried with the dead, reflecting the belief that they could guide the soul toward transformation and eternity.
By the Han Dynasty (206 BC-220 AD), silk had become both a pillar of imperial industry and a marker of cultural prestige. Vast state-run weaving workshops operated within the palace complexes of Chang'an (today's Shaanxi province), underscoring silk's central role in the empire's economy and political order. Weaving itself appears to have carried unusual social distinction. Historical accounts suggest that women associated with silk production could enjoy relatively elevated status, among them Consort Bo, mother of Emperor Wen, who was said to have once been a weaver before rising to imperial favor.
The technological sophistication behind this industry became increasingly visible during the Han period. In 2012, archaeologists excavating Han tombs in Chengdu, Sichuan province, uncovered four wooden loom models accompanied by painted figurines of textile workers, vividly illustrating the complexity and specialization of early Chinese weaving. At least one model has been identified as a drawloom capable of producing intricate patterned silks such as brocade, making it the earliest surviving physical model of its kind discovered anywhere in the world.
The discovery also underscored Sichuan province's long-standing role as a major silk production center. Decades of excavation at the Sanxingdui site in the province have yielded some of Bronze Age China's most iconic artifacts, including the towering Bronze Sacred Trees. According to Hu Jialin, the Shanghai Museum curator behind the well-publicized 2024 Sanxingdui exhibition, many scholars believe these extraordinary constructions represent Fusang, the mythical tree described in early texts as the resting place of the suns. Significantly, the character sang in Fusang means "mulberry" — the tree on which silkworms depend.
"One of the things revealed by silk archaeology is the interconnected yet plural foundations of Chinese civilization," Zhao said.
His view is reinforced by the discovery of several painted pottery spindle whorls dating back about 5,000 years across the central Yangtze River region — an area that would later give rise to the state of Chu (475-223 BC) and the broader Chu cultural tradition that long outlived it. Their varied forms, intricate patterns and carefully painted surfaces reveal a flourishing textile culture and, for some scholars, foreshadow the romantic, expansive spirit long associated with the Chu world.
That spirit found one of its finest expressions in silk. Unearthed in 1982 from an aristocratic Chu tomb at Mashan in Hubei province, a gauze garment embroidered with dragon, phoenix and tiger motifs remains one of the masterpieces of ancient Chinese textile art. Though nearly two meters long, it weighs only about 250 grams; its gauze is woven so delicately that it has often been compared to cicada wings. Upon this almost weightless surface, artisans embroidered intricate motifs using brilliantly colored silk threads finer than human hair. The tomb yielded dozens of silk textiles preserved in astonishing condition by the dense white clay sealing the burial chamber.
Separated by roughly two centuries yet linked by geography and cultural memory, the Mawangdui Han tombs in Changsha may be seen as heirs to this earlier Chu tradition.
Among the most exquisite textiles unearthed there is a form of luxury needlework known as Xinqi embroidery. Recorded by name in the funerary inventories, it was celebrated for its extraordinarily fine stitching, harmonious colors and motifs worked with almost microscopic precision. At first glance, its flowing tassel-like cloud patterns appear abstract, but scholars believe they are stylized swallows woven among curling vines and drifting clouds.
The symbolism was deeply resonant. Swallows are migratory birds that return unfailingly with the seasons, and the term xinqi — "faithful return" — alluded precisely to this cyclical homecoming. In the funerary imagination of Chu and early Han culture, the swallow became a metaphor for return itself: the return of spring, the return of memory, and perhaps even the hoped-for return of the soul.
