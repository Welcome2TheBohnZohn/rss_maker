ARTICLE TEXT:
It was already 10 am, yet the winter sun at this latitude had not been up long enough to bring much warmth to the ground. In the lingering cold inherited from last night, about 20 people had gathered halfway along Rue Américaine in Brussels, their bluish cigarette smoke mingling with the white clouds of their breath, making the wait a little more bearable.
Side A: An industry built on little things
They were waiting at the gate of Les Petits Riens, which means "the little nothings", a well-known Belgian secondhand shop founded in 1937. It accepts donations from the public, sorts and refurbishes them, and sells them as secondhand goods to support charitable causes.
For more than eight decades, it is precisely these little nothings, donated by generation after generation, that have kept the business going. According to figures released by Les Petits Riens, it received 8,867 metric tons of donated goods in 2025, equivalent to more than 24 tons every day. Previously published figures show that about 3.2 million secondhand items find new owners through its network annually.
With the goods themselves coming from donations, prices can be brought down to levels affordable even to those on a tight budget. There are plenty of handbags in decent condition, sitting in compartments and priced at just one euro a piece, while even those hanging on display racks cost only a few euros or slightly more than 10 euros, roughly the price of one of the cheapest meal deals at McDonald's.
Secondhand does not necessarily mean second-rate. Quite the contrary, in a society where donations are common and secondhand goods circulate efficiently, many everyday items enter the secondhand market long before they show serious signs of wear.
Here you can find well-maintained sneakers that are by no means out of fashion; clean and tidy stuffed toys that look little different from brand-new ones; and even wooden decorations that might once have cost quite a bit, allowing those with limited budgets to add a little charm to their homes.
On one shelf stood four decorative wooden letters spelling out "HOME" — Yes, in a European secondhand shop, you can even buy a "HOME". And behind that home lies an entire industry that creates a virtuous circle.
First, the environment benefits. A stuffed toy sitting in the window of a secondhand shop certainly does more good for the environment than one lying in a garbage bin, covered in rotten vegetable leaves and leftover soup.
Second, Europe's sizable low-income population stands to benefit. According to Eurostat, 72.4 million people, or 16.3 percent of the European Union population, were at risk of income poverty in 2025, meaning their equivalized disposable household income was below 60 percent of their national median. Figures released on Nov 17 further showed that about 28 million EU residents experienced severe material and social deprivation. Making desirable products affordable to those who are less well-off is a good move.
On the other hand, a well-functioning secondhand market also allows people to donate or resell things they no longer need, perhaps recovering some of their money while freeing up precious space in their increasingly more expensive homes.
Third, the industry creates jobs. Between being collected and appearing in a shop window, a secondhand item may go through sorting, cleaning, refurbishment, transportation and retailing, each of which creates jobs.
A study by Oxford Economics found that the secondhand clothing sector alone directly supported about 110,000 jobs across the EU, generating approximately 3 billion euros in direct gross value added. As a comparison, the BMW Group employs about 107,000 people across Europe.
In other words, the secondhand clothing sector alone supports more direct jobs than this global automotive giant employs in Europe. Add secondhand furniture, books and household goods, and one begins to appreciate how many livelihoods depend on this industry.
Even I could not resist buying something during the visit: a secondhand tripod for five euros. I put it to use right there in the shop — just see how well it works!
Side B: From thrift chic to thrift shame — and beyond
Yet as Europe's economy struggles to regain momentum, buying secondhand has quietly taken on a different meaning.
According to the European Commission's autumn economic forecast released on Nov 17, the EU's real GDP grew by 0.4 percent, 1.4 percent and 1.4 percent in 2023, 2024 and 2025 respectively, with growth projected at just 1.4 percent this year. Europe has thus spent nearly four years on a path of sluggish growth.
However, EU inflation is projected to reach 3.3 percent this year. European Commission figures show that between 2015 and 2025, overall consumer prices rose by 32.9 percent, while food prices climbed by 48 percent and those of electricity, gas and other fuels by 62.7 percent. In 2025, about 34 million EU residents spent more than 40 percent of their household disposable income on housing.
For an ordinary European, it's a major issue to count how much money is left in the wallet each month after paying the rent, settling the utility bills and buying groceries. That's also why so many Europeans have taken to the streets in protest this year — the people are angry and do you hear them sing?
Against this backdrop, buying secondhand is becoming, for some Europeans, less a matter of environmental awareness than a practical response to the rising cost of living.
Nora and Eve, a young couple I met in the shop, were browsing through handbags and clothes.
"Because it's more ecological, and in this case, it's also a social project," Eve said when asked why she chose to shop secondhand.
Nora added another consideration: "For me, it's ethical, and also because of the price."
More than a decade ago, buying secondhand was promoted as something fashionable. In 2009, German supermodel Heidi Klum and British singer Sophie Ellis-Bextor helped promote secondhand clothing for the charity Oxfam. In 2012, British actress Joanna Lumley fronted a campaign encouraging clothing reuse and even worked behind the till of a secondhand shop the following year.
The message was clear: Buying secondhand was not a sign of an empty wallet, but a statement of fashion, environmental awareness and social responsibility.
As financial pressures mounted around 2020, however, another perception of secondhand shopping became apparent — the embarrassment associated with buying secondhand for fear of being seen as financially struggling. Or maybe we can call it "secondhand shame" or "thrift shame".
The logic behind it is not difficult to understand: Those who can afford brand-new products can proudly tell their friends that they buy secondhand to protect the environment. Yet in comparison, those who buy secondhand because they cannot afford new products might find it a little harder to talk about that openly.
Just imagine the dramaticness at seeing that new thrift shame fading only several years after it emerged. That's not because economy is turning better, but because it's getting worse, and financial difficulties have become a more widely shared experience.
Gerrit Valere, an elderly gentleman I met in the shop, described this change in attitudes. A few years ago, he recalled, friends might have wondered whether someone visiting a secondhand shop was experiencing financial difficulties. Nowadays, with so many people turning up in secondhand shops themselves, there seems to be nothing unusual about it anymore.
Valere showed us his secondhand treasures: a suit jacket, trousers and a padded vest, among other items. For just over 30 euros, he could maintain his gentlemanly appearance, and he seemed quite pleased with the bargain.
It is good to see thrift shame fading. It's not good to see that financial pressure may be one of the reasons behind its disappearance.
It was in 1937 that the Les Petits Riens was built, when World War II had already broken out in Asia and Europe was still living in the shadow of the Great Depression, on the eve of another devastating war. Built on public donations and dedicated to giving back to society, the secondhand shop had played its role well for decades, even through the postwar reconstruction days and turned into a sign of environmentalism.
Its founders might not have thought that nearly 90 years later, amid another period of economic difficulty, it would once again become something people need lean on. It's not what they hope to see, either.
What European countries need to do now is reinvigorate their economies, so that one day people can walk into secondhand shops because they want to protect the environment, rather than because they cannot afford to shop elsewhere.
The ideal future of thrift shame is not simply one in which nobody feels ashamed of buying secondhand, but one in which everybody has the freedom to choose whether to buy secondhand.
Perhaps only then will the original vision of the shop's founders be fully realized.
