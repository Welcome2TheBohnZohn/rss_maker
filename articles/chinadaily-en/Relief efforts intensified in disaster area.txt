Until last month, Hao Zhen had only seen disaster relief efforts on the news. Then a devastating mudslide struck Gyirong, the remote border county in Xizang autonomous region where he works as a volunteer.
The 27-year-old was among 17 volunteers from China's Go West program who joined relief efforts in Gyirong township after the Aug 26 disaster.
While professional rescue teams searched the hardest-hit areas, Hao and his fellow volunteers worked behind the scenes, arranging accommodation, distributing supplies and helping provide meals for displaced residents and rescue workers.
"When the disaster first happened, a lot of affected residents and rescuers arrived in the township all at once," Hao said.
"The first thing we had to do was make sure they had somewhere to stay and food to eat."
As relief supplies poured in, the volunteers counted, sorted and moved thousands of boxes donated by Red Cross organizations, companies and foundations. They also helped with logistical work at the local emergency response headquarters and collected and organized information from the disaster area.
The experience gave Hao a new understanding of what disaster relief involves.
"Before this, I had only read news stories about volunteers helping after disasters," he said.
"Now I realize that you don't have to be on the search-and-rescue front line to make a difference. Keeping things running behind the scenes matters, too."
Born in 1999, Hao is the oldest of the 17 Go West program volunteers working in Gyirong township. Most of the others were born between 2000 and 2003. They come from across China, including Heilongjiang, Jiangxi and Shandong provinces, as well as Xizang, and have backgrounds ranging from medicine to education.
The work has come with practical challenges. Only two of the 17 volunteers speak Tibetan, creating communication difficulties at the shelters. Local township officials often stepped in to interpret.
Food and accommodation were also stretched in the first few days as large numbers of residents, rescuers and support workers arrived in the small border town. Hao said the situation gradually improved as local authorities coordinated with hotels and restaurants to provide additional support.
Another five Go West program volunteers remained in the county seat, helping receive relatives of missing people, assisting families and handling calls and follow-ups through the 12345 public service hotline. Volunteers from nearby counties, including Lhaze and Dingri, also came to help.
For Hao, Gyirong is a long way from home — nearly 5,700 kilometers from Shuangyashan in Heilongjiang province. He usually makes the journey home only once a year.
He arrived in Gyirong in February 2025 to join the Go West program and plans to stay for three years. His family initially tried to talk him out of the move, worried about the distance and the physical demands of living at high altitude.
"But I thought, if everyone worries about the hardship and the distance, places like this will have fewer and fewer young people willing to come," Hao said.
The physical challenges are real. When Hao first arrived in Gyirong, he found he did not have the same stamina he had at lower elevations. He also experienced heart palpitations when he first joined the relief effort. After more than a year on the plateau, however, he said his body has largely adapted.
His family still worries about him, particularly after learning that he was involved in the disaster response.
"But the disaster happened right here, where we work and live," Hao said.
"Since we chose to come here as volunteers, helping when something like this happens is simply part of what we should do."
The Aug 26 mudslide at Gyirong Port on the China-Nepal border was triggered by an ice-rock avalanche originating from a high-altitude glacier in Nepal. As of Friday evening, 31 people had been confirmed dead and 531 remained missing in Gyirong, according to the local emergency response and rescue command headquarters.
For Hao, the experience has also changed the way he sees the small tasks that fill much of his day.
Moving boxes, arranging accommodation and making sure people have meals may be far removed from the dramatic scenes of a search-and-rescue operation, but he no longer sees them as minor jobs.
"My biggest hope now is that we can keep the support work running smoothly, that all the rescuers return safely and that there are no more secondary disasters," he said.

China has deployed around 20 sets of monitoring equipment across the disaster-hit Gyirong area in the Xizang autonomous region, with real-time data showing geological conditions stabilizing, a senior geologist said.
The devices — including debris flow monitors, slope radars and microseismic sensors — were installed by the China Geological Survey and other emergency response teams following last month's ice-rock avalanche that buried parts of the Gyirong border crossing, causing massive casualties on both sides of the border.
Ge Daqing, deputy director of the China Aero Geophysical Survey and Remote Sensing Center for Natural Resources, said the equipment was positioned at key risk points across the disaster zone to monitor slope movement, debris flow channels and ground deformation in real time.
Two additional slope radars were installed on Wednesday near the border crossing after rescue teams cleared access roads, he added.
The devices are positioned to protect workers repairing roads and power lines by issuing early warnings if falling rocks or shallow landslides are detected on the slopes above.
Data collected so far indicates the dynamic processes at the site have entered a relatively stable phase. "Overall, there have been no significant fluctuations or changes," Ge said, adding that the risk of a large-scale secondary disaster is currently assessed as low without external triggers. However, he cautioned that high-risk factors persist due to the complex terrain.
Some minor rockfalls have been observed at the glacier front in the collapse zone, but Ge said these pose no immediate threat.
The monitoring equipment is primarily intended for the emergency response phase. Once a comprehensive risk assessment is completed and no major threats remain, the devices may be withdrawn, he said.
Beyond the immediate response, the disaster has underscored the limitations of existing monitoring technologies in the high-altitude, cloud-prone Himalayan region.
Ge said optical satellite imagery — the most common remote sensing tool — is severely hampered by persistent cloud cover and fog in the area. Clear images can be obtained on only about 20 days a year, and most of the optical data received recently have been obscured by clouds.
To address this, Ge called for the accelerated development of new synthetic aperture radar satellites, particularly an interferometric SAR satellite constellation that combines L-band, high-resolution, wide-swath capabilities, and both sun-synchronous and low-inclination orbits. This can overcome the challenge of monitoring rapid deformation in the high-elevation mountains and glaciers of the Himalayas.
He also stressed the need to improve satellite revisit cycles and spatial resolution to enable more frequent and rapid monitoring.
Unmanned aerial vehicles offer another monitoring option, but current drones struggle to operate above 7,000 meters and their sensors remain vulnerable to cloud interference. New radar-based sensors and improved high-altitude flight capabilities are needed, he added.
Looking ahead, Ge warned that the risk of high-altitude ice-rock avalanches in the region is likely to increase.
"The 2015 Nepal earthquake caused internal damage to these mountain masses," he said.
"Combined with glacial erosion at high elevations and steep terrain, the structural integrity of rock formations has been compromised, making them prone to collapse."
Given the transboundary nature of such hazards, Ge highlighted the importance of international collaboration in disaster monitoring and early warning across the Himalayan region, noting that ice-rock avalanches and glacial lake outburst floods do not respect national borders.

Medical and epidemic prevention personnel from the Chinese military have begun testing soil samples from the core area of the mudslide-hit Gyirong Port in Shigatse, Xizang autonomous region, to help formulate disinfection and personal protection plans as detailed search and rescue efforts continue.
Bacterial culture and pathogen testing are being used to gauge the risk of infectious diseases for rescue crews working amid shattered rocks and thick sludge in the core area, said a medical and disease prevention team with the People's Liberation Army Xizang Military Command's logistics support department.
The road access was largely restored on Wednesday morning.
Lhamo Tsering, a military technician specializing in infectious disease prevention and control, said that soil samples collected from the core zone would go through testing for 21 vector-borne pathogens, 23 respiratory pathogens and 32 gastrointestinal pathogens.
"Conducting pathogen testing on these soil samples can identify the types and levels of soil pathogen contamination, assess the risk of secondary infectious diseases, trace the sources of pollution, and guide environmental disinfection and personnel protection," she said.
"The results can also help us evaluate the effectiveness of epidemic control efforts and prevent the spread of soil-borne infectious diseases after a disaster," she added.
Liu Xiaonan, a member of the team, was among the first to enter the core zone on Wednesday after the road leading to the port reopened. He recalled heavy downpours that evening, and his team worked until late before returning.
One of the most challenging parts of his work was navigating the deep mud in the area. "In some areas, the sludge looks solid, but when I try walking on it, my feet can get sucked down until the mud comes up to my calves or even my knees. Sometimes we are only able to pull ourselves out with the help of nearby personnel," he said.
It is also important to prevent mosquito and other insect bites in the area, he added. Lhamo Tsering was one of the two female military personnel working on-site on Friday morning. The other was tasked with providing psychological consulting for search and rescue crew members.
Lhamo Tsering said she had participated in responding to acute health emergencies, including the COVID-19 outbreak, in the past. It was her first time working on the front line in the aftermath of a devastating natural disaster of this scale.
"I am used to performing tests in the laboratory using samples collected and transported back by my fellow soldiers. This time, I asked to come to the hardest-hit area because I wanted to collect the samples myself to ensure the precision of my work," she said.
As a native of Lhasa, the regional capital of Xizang, Lhamo Tsering said she was also deeply saddened by the havoc wreaked by the mudslide at Gyirong Port. "This area is naturally beautiful with pristine air, a comfortable altitude and lush vegetation," she said. "I hope that one day it can restore its beauty."

After days of relentless search and rescue efforts, Xiao Fugui, a police officer at the entry-exit border inspection station in Gyirong county, Shigatse, Xizang autonomous region, looked gaunt and exhausted.
Despite his weary appearance, he insisted repeatedly that he was not tired at all. "All I want is to find my workmates," he said on Thursday, gripping an iron shovel with determination, his brow furrowed.
On Aug 26, a massive mudslide devastated Gyirong Port, a vital land link between China and Nepal. By Wednesday noon, 21 victims had been found, 847 personal belongings recovered, and 541 people remained missing.
Xiao was driving toward the port when the disaster struck. "I was just four or five kilometers away when I saw several cars pulled over," he said. "I got out and asked other drivers what was happening — everyone was panicked, saying the water level had risen sharply near the port."
Concerned for their safety, he instructed the two female colleagues in his car to get out and find a safe place, then continued driving alone to assess the situation. Upon realizing it was a landslide, Xiao immediately alerted the authorities.
"The muddy water was rushing toward me, so I threw the car into reverse and backed up about 500 meters before stopping," he recounted, his voice tense. "I was terrified the car would be swept away. What really scared me was not knowing what had happened to my workmates at the port."
As visibility dropped sharply and the road ahead was blocked, Xiao found himself trapped. Reflecting on that moment, he said, "I really walked through the gates of hell that day."
Driven by worry and a deep sense of duty, Xiao joined the rescue teams. By Sunday, he had reached the vicinity of the port and immediately immersed himself in the relief efforts. "As soon as I arrived, I started moving supplies, then began searching. I found clothing, shoes and crushed vehicles belonging to the missing. Every single item I saw pierced my heart like a needle."
Originally from Qinghai province, the 39-year-old Xiao has worked in Xizang for 17 years, with 11 of those years at the Gyirong entry-exit border inspection station, primarily handling publicity affairs. "For many years, I've spent almost every day with my workmates — filming their patrols, writing about their lives guarding this border," he said. "This port, these people — they've become part of me."
On the first day of the rescue, Xiao and other workers camped in a tent by a reservoir halfway up the mountain near the border port. He did not sleep at all. "The rain was so heavy that night — water poured down the slopes and merged into the river. The sound of rain, rushing water, and collapsing hillsides all mixed together. It made my heart sink. I didn't dare to sleep, and I couldn't sleep anyway," he said.
As he spoke, he picked up the shovel and resumed digging. "I have only one thought: no matter what, I want to find my workmates. I want to bring them home," he added.

Zhangmu Port in Nyalam of Shigatse, Xizang autonomous region, will adjust its customs and border inspection facilities from Saturday to improve safety management for the high-altitude mountain environment, according to an announcement by the local government on Thursday.
Located in a deep Himalayan valley, the port is a major land gateway between China and Nepal. Its mountainous terrain exposes the area to geological hazards.
The adjustment is aimed at protecting inbound and outbound travelers and their property, while maintaining normal border clearance operations and orderly movement through the port.
Starting at 10 am on Saturday, customs and immigration inspection operations at Zhangmu Port will be moved to the port's former temporary inspection area for import cargo.
The road section connecting the Friendship Bridge border gate to the new inspection site will be designated as a controlled port area and a customs transit supervision zone, with closed-loop management implemented.
Under the new arrangement, cross-border travelers will be transferred through designated routes. They will not be allowed to leave the designated area, move around freely, get on and off vehicles midway, or have unauthorized contact with people outside the designated transfer system.
Without permission, vehicles will not be allowed to stop along the controlled section to pick up or drop off passengers or load or unload goods. Unrelated personnel and vehicles will also be barred from entering the area.
Authorities said that the change only concerns the layout of customs and border inspection facilities. The port's opening status and bilateral customs clearance policies will remain unchanged.

Twenty-one people have been confirmed dead, and 541 remain missing after a mudslide struck Gyirong Port on the China-Nepal border in Xizang autonomous region on Aug 26, according to the regional government's latest news briefing.
The mudslide began with the collapse of a high-altitude glacier in Nepal, which triggered an ice-rock avalanche that developed into a fast-moving debris flow before reaching the port. The disaster has brought renewed attention to the Himalayan valley in which Gyirong sits.
Gyirong is one of the main land gateways between China and Nepal. Its geography made it a natural passage between the Qinghai-Xizang Plateau and South Asia. It has been used for more than 1,000 years. Long before the establishment of a modern border crossing, the route carried royal missions, diplomats, religious masters, and traders.
In the seventh century, Nepali Princess Bhrikuti traveled through Gyirong on her way to marry Tibetan King Songtsan Gampo. The Buddha statue she was said to have brought was enshrined at Phakpa Lhakhang Temple in modern-day Gyirong county.
The same century also saw Tang Dynasty (618-907) envoy Wang Xuance pass through the area on a mission to India in 658. Wang left a cliff inscription as a record of the journey that survives today.
In the eighth century, Indian Buddhist master Padmasambhava also traveled through Gyirong, which was described as the first stop on his mission, as he entered Xizang to teach Buddhism.
Together, these journeys illustrate Gyirong's long-standing role as a corridor for exchanges between the plateau and South Asia. That role continued into modern times.
Gyirong was designated a national first-class land port in 1978 and opened to travelers from third countries in 2017, becoming an important gateway for cross-border travel and trade. The port saw imports and exports worth 4.25 billion yuan ($632.6 million) in 2024, ranking first among ports in the region and accounting for nearly 30 percent of the total China-Nepal trade volume, according to Xinhua News Agency.

The risk of secondary disasters remains a major concern. More rain is forecast in Gyirong over the next three days, according to the China Meteorological Administration.
Three national emergency rescue teams, comprising 39 personnel, have formed a joint monitoring group, the Ministry of Emergency Management said. The teams have accumulated 120 hours of monitoring and collected about 2,700 sets of data on barrier lakes, landslide areas and unstable slopes.
Rockfall radar, slope radar and other equipment are monitoring the surrounding mountains, gullies and debris deposits around the clock. Meteorological authorities are providing detailed forecasts every six hours.
Yao said rescuers have been instructed to prioritize safety, with teams withdrawing from low-lying valley areas at night and operating in groups with rope protection. Personnel have been told to evacuate immediately if monitoring systems detect signs of danger.
Chinese meteorological and water authorities have stepped up cooperation with Nepal following the deadly mudslide that struck the two countries' border, sharing monitoring data and providing weather and hydrological forecasts to support rescue efforts.
The Aug 26 disaster originated on the Nepalese side of the border and severely affected Gyirong Port in the Xizang autonomous region. The area's high altitude, complex terrain and rapidly changing weather conditions have made rescue operations particularly challenging.
"From late August to early September, plateau shear lines and low vortices are frequently active along the border, with frequent precipitation, posing serious challenges to rescue work," said Ma Xuekuan, chief forecaster at the National Meteorological Center.
Following a request from Nepal's Department of Hydrology and Meteorology around noon on Aug 26, the China Meteorological Administration organized an emergency joint consultation with its Nepalese counterpart that afternoon. A second consultation was held on Aug 31, according to Zhang Tianhang, a senior engineer at the World Meteorological Centre Beijing.
The two sides also established a WeChat working group for real-time information sharing, allowing them to respond quickly to requests during the emergency.
The consultations used MAZU, a Chinese cloud-based early warning system, to jointly assess weather conditions and forecast precipitation in the affected area.
China subsequently established a dedicated meteorological support team for Nepal and began providing daily monitoring and forecasting reports in English.
"Overall, we have provided seamless forecasting services covering the next 30 days, offering strong support for Nepal's rescue operations," Zhang said.
China has also provided Nepal with a customized version of MAZU. Nepal has since requested additional functions, including lightning monitoring, subseasonal to seasonal forecasts and more detailed monitoring and forecasting products.
Chinese authorities have shared a range of meteorological products and observational data through the customized version of MAZU, Zhang said.
Water authorities have also joined the effort.
A barrier lake formed near the confluence of Nepal's Cuojian River and China's Purepu Tsangpo River following the disaster. The Ministry of Water Resources immediately sent a hydrological emergency monitoring team to the area, working with Xizang's regional hydrological and water resources survey authorities.
Chinese authorities are providing Nepal with daily updates in five areas, including the development of the barrier lake; potential chain risks from impact pits along the Cuojian River; risks from glacial lakes in the Gyirong Zangbo basin; the development and risk forecasts of newly formed barrier lakes; and emergency hydrological monitoring information.
The information is intended to help Nepal assess risks and organize rescue efforts downstream, according to the ministry.
Following the disaster, the CMA mobilized resources from the national to local levels. A small ground weather station was installed near the disaster area on the night of Aug 26 and a mobile weather radar was deployed nearby on Sept 1.
"These facilities have supplemented our understanding of local weather conditions and improved the precision and accuracy of local forecasts," Ma said.
