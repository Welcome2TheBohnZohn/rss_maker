Marriage registrations get new venues
Changes part of policy innovations to address decline in willingness to wed
Authorities are extending marriage registration services to nontraditional venues — from a bank branch in Tianjin to a maternity care hospital in Shandong province — as part of policy innovations to streamline procedures at a time when the country is facing record low marriage numbers.
In early August, the Donghai marriage registration office in Tianjin opened inside a postal savings bank branch, the first such counter inside a financial institution, local media reported.
The renovated branch features a romance-themed hub where couples can send postcards to themselves scheduled for delivery seven years later and order personalized debit cards printed with their wedding photos. Beyond the perks, the new venue offers convenience for engaged couples who often need to sort out financial arrangements before marriage.
In Zibo, Shandong, Qidu Hospital, a maternal and children's health hospital, launched on-site marriage registration ahead of Qixi Festival, China's traditional Valentine's Day, which fell on Aug 19 this year.
The integrated counter, on the hospital's third floor, allows newlyweds to complete premarital health checks, prepregnancy counseling and folic acid pickups in a single visit.
After registration, couples can directly get health checks and fertility consultations without running around — "it saves worry and feels thoughtful", the hospital said in a social media post.
The unconventional venues are not limited to Tianjin and Shandong. In Hefei, Anhui province, an office opened inside Xingfuba subway station — whose name translates as "happiness dam" — in January 2024. By late July, it had processed more than 6,000 marriage applications, making it a popular local wedding destination thanks to its symbolic name.
At a highway service area venue in Shijiazhuang, Hebei province, a registration office opened on June 30 beside the "0 km" starting marker of the city's ring greenway, a spot popular with cyclists.
Officials are using the landmark's symbolism to frame marriage as the start of a new journey.
Local governments in other places have also experimented with extending registration services to music festivals, tourist vessels, scenic spots, shopping malls and mountain tops.
The push for more accessible registration is part of China's promarriage policies.
In May 2025, the government updated national rules, scrapping the household registration booklet requirement and allowing nationwide interprovincial marriage registration, so that couples can marry anywhere without returning to their official hometowns.
In its first year, the new rule facilitated 682,000 cross-region registrations.
Other supportive measures include more than 4,747 marriage and family counseling rooms established nationwide, covering all county-level and higher registration institutions and staffed by an 18,000-strong team, over half with professional psychology or social work certifications.
Zhejiang province has combined marriage registration, birth registration and household status modification into a one-stop application system, which has handled 1.52 million integrated cases to date.
The moves come as marriage registrations in the first half of 2026 dropped to their lowest level since at least 2014.
Marriage registrations in China in the first half of 2026 fell to a record low of 3.28 million, a retreat from the modest recovery made in 2025 over the previous year and marking the weakest January-June tally since 2014, for which period comparable data is available.
Demographers attribute the sustained decline to structural demographic and social shifts, such as evolving marital expectations and growing female independence.
Administrative simplification alone has failed to reverse the long-term decline, experts say.
"These changes improve procedural efficiency but do not address the core reluctance among young people to marry," said Yang Yifan, a professor at Southwest Jiaotong University, who serves on the expert committee of the China National Working Commission on Aging.
He said venue expansions and rule revisions only solve logistical barriers, not the root causes. To reverse the trend, he advocated comprehensive life cycle support including housing subsidies, childcare stipends and expanded public nursery resources.
Zhang Jing, a family studies expert at Beijing University of Technology's Gengdan Institute, said, "Recent innovations such as the bank-based wedding counters may deliver a short-term lift in willingness to wed, and signal steady progress on population and family welfare work."
To foster a more marriage-friendly environment, Zhang called for family culture and mental health education for adolescents, transitional public housing benefits for newlyweds, and fulltime community social service roles focused on family well-being.
