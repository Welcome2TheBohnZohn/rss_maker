TITLE:
China's resilience exposes limits of US tariff policy

ARTICLE TEXT:
A tariff intended to exert pressure abroad is still a tax collected at home. The Sino-US trade dispute has illustrated the difficulty of imposing costs on a major economic partner without imposing them on one's own firms and consumers.
China's resilience has exposed the limits of a tariff diplomacy. Resilience matters, but its lasting value depends on translating industrial strength into broadly shared prosperity at home and building more stable economic relationships abroad. Both require continued domestic reform and sustained international cooperation.
September's agreement to extend the trade truce to Jan 10 offers an opening. The two governments also advanced a framework for more favorable tariff treatment covering approximately $30 billion of goods in each direction, subject to domestic legal procedures. Differences over technology, industrial policy and economic security will require sustained consultation.
An assessment of tariff diplomacy begins with who bears its costs. Research published in October by economists at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York and Columbia University found that nearly 90 percent of the tariff increases studied passed through to tariff-inclusive US import prices, using data through February 2026.
The burden is subsequently distributed through business margins, supply chains and retail prices. US manufacturers relying on imported components face higher costs alongside their customers. Protection conferred on one industry can impose a disadvantage on another.
Tariffs can redirect purchases and shelter particular producers. Lasting competitiveness, however, requires skills, technology, infrastructure and suppliers. Raising the price of competing imports cannot, by itself, bring these capabilities into existence.
China's manufacturing strength, above all, rests on an ecosystem accumulated over decades. Supplier networks, skilled workers, engineering expertise, infrastructure and production at scale reinforce one another. Their interaction helps firms resolve production problems and bring new products to market. A factory may be replaced more readily than the knowledge and relationships that make it productive.
Chinese firms have also diversified markets and reorganized production. Final assembly abroad may continue to depend on Chinese machinery, materials and components. Bilateral trade figures reveal only part of this adjustment.
Innovation in batteries, renewable energy equipment and digital technologies adds resilience through improvements in quality, efficiency and performance.
The interaction between research, engineering and production also creates opportunities for innovation across borders. Appropriate commercial incentives and regulatory conditions could help both economies develop and apply new technologies more efficiently through cooperation.
Changing production patterns nevertheless entail adjustment. IMD Business School Professor Richard Baldwin's recent analysis highlights the importance of scale: slower growth from a much larger industrial base can still generate substantial additions to output. The benefits and adjustments associated with that expansion need not be evenly distributed. Cheaper imports can benefit consumers and downstream firms, while particular industries and communities face more concentrated pressures.
Concerns about employment and industrial capabilities therefore deserve greater attention in both countries. Workers need support and training, while firms need room to adapt and investment to improve productivity. Given the scale of China's industrial base, sustained industrial success is increasingly tied to the opportunities it creates across economies. Continued dialogue and practical cooperation with trading partners can help those opportunities develop.
For China, sustaining resilience requires continued domestic reform. The capacity to produce, the income available to households and the jobs available to workers need to advance together. A stronger domestic market depends on bringing them into closer alignment.
Recent data reveal the unevenness of demand. In the first nine months of this year, retail sales of goods rose by 1 percent in nominal terms, while retail sales of services increased by 5 percent. Services offer an important avenue for growth, but stronger household purchasing power remains essential to a broader expansion of demand.
Yi Gang, former governor of the People's Bank of China, has emphasized support for young children, better schooling and affordable housing for migrant worker families, and better access to healthcare. These proposals address the longer perspective of household decisions: what must be reserved for future limits what can be spent today.
Yu Yongding, a prominent scholar at the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences, approaches weak demand from another direction. When households await higher incomes and firms await more orders, public infrastructure investment can help break the impasse. It can generate employment, improve business conditions and, by lowering costs, encourage private investment.
The enduring case for a public investment project rests partly on the economic activity it makes possible after construction is complete. Its immediate contribution to demand should be assessed alongside its effects on productivity, employment and subsequent incomes. The quality of investment determines how far today's expenditure strengthens tomorrow's economy.
Yi's discussion of local government assets draws a useful distinction between an asset's social value and the cash flow available to service its debt. Public investment needs arrangements for repayment and continued operation. Combining capital investment with sustainable funding for public services can support employment, ease household financial pressures and translate public assets into lasting gains in living standards and consumption.
Employment policy must also respond to technological change. Harvard Professor Dani Rodrik has argued that China should extend its experience in raising manufacturing productivity to services. Helping smaller service-sector firms improve technology, management and quality can spread productivity gains more widely. More predictable regulations and fairer market access can strengthen private firms' confidence to invest and hire.
Economists differ over the roles of income distribution, investment and exchange rates in rebalancing. These debates underline the macroeconomic roots of trade tensions. Tariff negotiations alone cannot address all of them. Trade measures can rearrange bilateral flows without resolving the underlying balance between saving and investment. Lasting adjustments depend partly on domestic choices in economies.
Successful domestic adjustments would also strengthen international cooperation. More secure household purchasing power would give Chinese firms a larger home market and foreign businesses new opportunities, supported by transparent and dependable market access. A stronger home market would also give trading partners more scope to benefit from China's growth and accommodate the changes it brings.
The rules governing international engagement therefore matter. Competition increasingly concerns subsidies, technical standards, data governance and market access. These arrangements influence investment decisions and determine how firms manage interdependence.
Practical cooperation could begin with simpler customs procedures, electronic trade documents and clearer guidance on product classification and origin.
Subsidies require greater transparency and regular consultation. Reliable information about objectives, beneficiaries and cross-border effects would permit a fuller assessment of the interests of competing producers, downstream businesses and consumers. Evidence cannot reconcile every policy preference, but it can establish more intelligible grounds for disagreement.
Such efforts should build on multilateral rules and remain open to other economies. Security measures need clearly defined purposes and should be proportionate to identifiable risks, preserving sufficient certainty for ordinary commerce, investment and scientific exchanges.
Universities and research institutions can bring analytical discipline to debates on supply chains, technology and trade. Sustained exchanges among scholars, businesses and policymakers can distinguish disagreements about evidence from differences of interest, and clarify where accommodation remains possible.
A stable Sino-US economic relationship should make adjustments more manageable and prosperity more widely shared. China's resilience offers a firmer basis for domestic reform, while greater predictability would give firms and households in both economies more confidence to invest and plan. Economic strength is ultimately measured by the prosperity it sustains, not the pressure it can exert.
The writer is a professor at the University of International Business and Economics.
