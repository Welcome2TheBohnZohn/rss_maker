TITLE:
From punk to rap, women upend cliches

ARTICLE TEXT:
"Yesterday, another one of my girlfriends died.
The day before, we were still blaming her for not being there, saying we couldn't get a full table of mahjong without her. One by one, they've all gone … I'm terribly afraid of dying."
The words are delivered in a strong regional accent over a rap beat by a woman in her 60s. There is no cool music video, choreographed performance or manufactured hip-hop image. Instead, she speaks openly about death, friendship and the people disappearing from her life.
The song, Yesterday, Another One of My Girlfriends Died, is the work of Gui Xiang, a 62-year-old woman from Suzhou, Jiangsu province, who has found an unlikely fan base through rap.
Her unconventional performances are growing in popularity on Chinese social media, where her age, raw delivery, accent and irreverent humor defy images usually associated with hip-hop artists. She has 215,000 followers on Xiaohongshu (Red-Note) and 575,000 on Douyin, where her videos have accumulated more than 12.35 million likes.
Gui is part of a growing cohort of middle-aged and older Chinese women indulging in unexpected musical roles, from rappers and rock musicians to singers who have turned public parks into their stages.
Their stories point to a broader shift in how some Chinese women are approaching later stages of life. Instead of restricting their leisure activities to familiar pursuits, such as square dancing in public parks, practicing tai chi or doing morning exercises and looking after grandchildren, they are experimenting with fresh artistic expression, including genres and subcultures traditionally associated with younger people.
A growing interest in tech skills and an appetite for late-life learning are also improving their capabilities to pursue new interests. The 2025 New Silver Generation Lifestyle Report, published by Beijing News, found that more than half of the respondents — those aged over 60 — had attended or were attending interest classes or training courses. Nearly one-fifth were learning new skills, ranging from using digital devices and photography to handicrafts and short-video editing.
The report also found that short-video platforms have become an important part of older people's lives, with nearly 60 percent of respondents using platforms such as Douyin and Kuaishou.
Sociologist Zhang Hexuan, who holds a doctorate from University College London, said this generation is entering old age equipped with new digital tools and skills.
They have diverse sources of information and a strong desire to explore, he said, while retaining a preference for social interaction and collective activities.
"Personal growth and a sense of belonging to a group are developing simultaneously in this generation," he said.
China's community networks provide a foundation for the more varied, multi-interest lifestyles they are seeking, Zhang added.
